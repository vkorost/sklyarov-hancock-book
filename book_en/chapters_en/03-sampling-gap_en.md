# Chapter 3: The Sampling Gap

In 1984, a thirty-two-year-old Australian physician named Barry Marshall scraped a culture of Helicobacter pylori from a patient’s stomach biopsy, grew it in a petri dish, and drank it.

He did this because nobody believed him. Marshall and his colleague Robin Warren had spent two years arguing that stomach ulcers were caused by a bacterium, not by stress, not by spicy food, not by the accumulated anxieties of modern life. The medical establishment was not interested. Gastroenterologists had built their careers on the acid-production model of ulcer disease. The pharmaceutical industry had built a profitable drug category around it. And Marshall, who was not a gastroenterologist but a trainee in internal medicine at Royal Perth Hospital, lacked the institutional standing to force a conversation.

The problem was not that his claim was unfalsifiable. It was trivially falsifiable. You culture the bacterium, you give it to a healthy subject, and you see if the subject develops gastritis. If not, Marshall is wrong. The experiment was straightforward. The ethics board would not approve it. No gastroenterologist would refer a patient for what they considered a crackpot trial. The test that would settle the question was available in every hospital microbiology lab in the world, and it was institutionally blocked.

So Marshall infected himself. He developed acute gastritis within five days. He confirmed the bacterial colonization by endoscopy. He treated himself with antibiotics and bismuth and recovered. He published the results in 1985. It took another decade for the medical establishment to accept what the experiment had shown. Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005, twenty-three years after the petri dish.

I bring up Barry Marshall not because his story parallels the world of alternative archaeology in every detail. It does not. Marshall was a trained physician working within a hospital system; Hancock and Sklyarov are outsiders without laboratory credentials. Marshall’s self-experiment, while reckless, produced clear binary results; the tests that Hancock and Sklyarov want performed would produce data requiring interpretation. The cases are different in important ways.

But they share one structural feature, and it is the feature that matters for this chapter: the test that would resolve the argument exists, both sides know what it is, and it has not been performed. Not because the test is technically impossible. Not because the result would be ambiguous. But because the people who control access to the test material do not consider the question worth asking.

In 2014, Andrey Sklyarov published his twenty-eighth book. It was about Easter Island.

The conventional story of Easter Island is well known. A Polynesian people settled the island around 800 to 1200 AD, carved the moai statues from volcanic tuff at the Rano Raraku quarry, transported them to coastal platforms called ahu, and then, through some combination of resource depletion and social collapse, stopped building and began toppling their own monuments. The story has become a parable of civilizational hubris. Jared Diamond made it the centerpiece of his book Collapse. It is the cautionary tale that environmentalists reach for when they want to explain what happens when a society exceeds its carrying capacity.

Sklyarov went to Easter Island and found something that did not fit the parable.

On one of the pukao (the red scoria cylinders that sit on the moai’s heads like hats), his team found a mark. It was on pukao number thirty-nine, and reaching it required stepping off the narrow tourist path, which led to a confrontation with a park guard. The mark was a clean, straight cut in stone, with the geometry of a disk-saw pass. Not a chisel mark. Not a pecking trace. A saw cut. In stone that the Rapa Nui people are supposed to have shaped with basalt hand tools.

Sklyarov photographed it. He measured the kerf width. He noted the absence of the conchoidal fracture pattern that stone-on-stone percussion produces. And then he wrote something that appears in the methodology section of the Easter Island book with an honesty unusual for any author, mainstream or alternative.

He wrote that the most powerful test of his claims would be chemical and microscopic analysis of the stone surfaces where anomalous marks appear. Specifically: residue analysis of the saw kerf to identify what material cut the stone. Surface chemistry at sites where he suspected the “plasticine technology” (his term for a hypothesized process that temporarily softened stone to a workable consistency). Microscopic examination of crystal structure at the stone surface for evidence of thermal or chemical modification.

He specified what the test would look like. He specified what results would confirm his hypothesis (unusual chemical compounds in surface residues, amorphous crystal structure at the treated surface distinct from the crystalline structure below it). He specified what results would refute it (no chemical difference between surface and interior, no anomalous residue in the kerf).

Then he said he could not perform these tests. The site is a UNESCO World Heritage property managed by CONAF, the Chilean environmental authority, and the Rapa Nui Council. No one from the Laboratory of Alternative History, which was not an accredited academic institution, was going to receive permission to take samples from the moai or the pukao. The guard who confronted his team for stepping off the path was not going to hand over a stone sample.

This is the moment where the story stops being about Easter Island and starts being about something larger.

I want to give this phenomenon a name, because it appears again and again in the work of both researchers, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

The Sampling Gap is the distance between a testable claim and the test that would resolve it, when that distance is maintained not by technical impossibility but by institutional access. The claim is specific. The test is known. The technology to perform the test exists and, in most cases, is routine. What does not exist is permission.

The Sampling Gap is not unique to alternative archaeology. It appears wherever questions about sensitive material require physical access controlled by institutions that do not consider the questions legitimate. Cancer researchers cannot test drugs on humans without FDA approval. Paleontologists cannot excavate certain fossil beds without landowner consent. Geneticists cannot sequence certain ancient DNA without museum cooperation. In each case, the gap between “we could test this” and “we are allowed to test this” is maintained by a gatekeeper whose incentives may or may not align with the researcher’s.

What makes the Sampling Gap unusual in the case of Hancock and Sklyarov is its scope. It is not one test at one site. It is a systematic absence of testing across dozens of sites on multiple continents, extending over decades, covering some of the most visited and studied archaeological monuments on Earth.

Consider what has never been tested.

The saw kerfs at Giza. Petrie documented them in 1883. Sklyarov documented additional examples across multiple Egyptian sites over a decade of expeditions. The kerfs are visible, photographable, measurable. The question of what tool material produced them is answerable with a scanning electron microscope and an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer. You scrape a microgram of residue from inside the kerf, you bombard it with electrons, and the machine tells you what elements are present. If the residue contains copper, the conventional explanation (copper saws with sand abrasive) gains support. If it contains diamond, carbide, or an alloy outside the ancient metallurgical repertoire, the conventional explanation has a problem.

This test takes about forty-five minutes of machine time. The equipment is standard in any university materials science department. The amount of sample required is invisible to the naked eye.

It has never been performed by any mainstream archaeological institution on any ancient Egyptian saw kerf.

Sklyarov cited one set of SEM results, conducted by researchers whose names he gives (including geological work by Julia Gorlova and metallurgical analysis in collaboration with Sergei Grigoriev), showing zero copper particles and zero tin particles in ancient saw kerfs at Egyptian sites. Roman-era kerfs from the same sites did contain copper residue. The null result at the ancient sites was, in his reading, diagnostic: whatever cut those stones, it was not a copper saw.

These results were not published in a peer-reviewed journal. They were published in Sklyarov’s books, in Russian, with limited analytical detail. No independent laboratory has replicated them. The reason they have not been replicated is not that the analytical method is exotic. SEM-EDS is one of the most common analytical techniques in materials science. The reason is that no mainstream laboratory has been motivated to ask the question, and no alternative researcher has been granted the access to provide the sample.

Now consider the stone vessels from beneath the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, the thirty thousand objects described in Chapter 1: diorite, schist, basalt, quartzite, with walls as thin as one and a half millimeters, shapes that no known tool can account for. Both Hancock and Sklyarov treated these vessels as among the strongest evidence for a technology outside the conventional Egyptian toolkit. The manufacturing impossibility was common ground between them.

Here is the test that has not been performed. Take one of the thirty thousand vessels. Examine its interior surface under a metallurgical microscope at high magnification. Look for tool marks: their geometry tells you what kind of tool made them. Rotary marks (concentric, regular spacing) indicate a lathe. Linear marks (parallel, axial) indicate a reciprocating tool. Irregular marks (random orientation, variable depth) indicate hand tools. The mark geometry is diagnostic. It is the same analysis that any forensic examiner performs on a bullet recovered from a crime scene: the marks tell you what touched the surface.

Then take a surface sample. Not a large one. A milligram. Run it through an SEM. Look for embedded particles from the cutting tool: diamond, silicon carbide, tungsten, whatever was harder than the stone being worked. If the particles match known ancient materials (copper, bronze, flint), the conventional explanation holds. If they match industrial abrasives, you have a different conversation.

The vessels have been in museum collections for over a century. They are among the most written-about artifacts in Egyptology. The test would damage the surface less than a museum visitor’s fingerprint damages a painting. It has not been done. Not because anyone disputes the vessels are anomalous (the interior geometries are acknowledged as puzzling even in mainstream sources). But because the question the test would answer is not a question the mainstream considers worth asking. The answer, whichever way it went, would either confirm a position that no Egyptologist wants to occupy (the conventional toolset is inadequate) or provide ammunition to researchers the mainstream does not wish to engage.

There is a pattern here, and it is worth naming explicitly.

The mainstream critique of Hancock and Sklyarov, the critique you hear in any academic seminar or archaeology podcast where their names come up, has a specific structure. It goes like this: their claims are unfalsifiable. They propose a lost civilization (or an extraterrestrial presence) and then explain away the absence of evidence by pointing to destruction, submersion, or institutional refusal to look. This makes their hypothesis immune to disproof. It is not science. It is a belief system dressed in the vocabulary of inquiry.

This critique is important and it deserves respect. Unfalsifiability is a genuine epistemological problem. A claim that cannot, in principle, be shown to be wrong is not a scientific claim. And Hancock in particular has been forthright about the difficulty: his proposed lost civilization occupied coastal zones now underwater, lived in regions now under jungle canopy or desert sand, and may have used organic materials that do not survive in the archaeological record. The absence of evidence is explained by the hypothesis itself, which is circular.

But the Sampling Gap complicates this critique in a way that the critics rarely acknowledge.

Several of Sklyarov’s claims are not unfalsifiable. They are trivially falsifiable. He specified, in his own books, exactly what tests would falsify them. The saw kerf residue test. The stone vessel tool-mark analysis. The surface chemistry test for the plasticine technology. The crystal structure examination at treated stone surfaces. Each of these tests produces binary results. Either the anomalous residue is there or it is not. Either the tool marks match known ancient tools or they do not.

The unfalsifiability, in these cases, is not in the hypothesis. It is in the access.

There is a difference between a claim that cannot be tested and a claim that has not been tested. The first is an epistemological problem. The second is a sociological one. Sklyarov was clear about which one he faced. He wrote, across multiple books, that the gap between what the evidence implied and what available analytical methods could directly confirm was “the honest central limitation” of his body of work, and he identified it as the primary obstacle to mainstream acceptance.

The tests he specified were never performed.

Hancock faces a version of the same gap, though his version has a different shape.

Hancock’s evidence base is less dependent on laboratory analysis and more dependent on field investigation of uninvestigated regions. His central claim is that approximately twenty-seven million square kilometers of continental shelf, submerged by post-glacial sea-level rise, have not been adequately surveyed for archaeological remains. He cites figures: less than five percent of this area has been studied. The Sahara, nine million square kilometers, is almost entirely unexcavated. The Amazon, five to six million square kilometers under canopy, is just beginning to yield its secrets through LiDAR.

His version of the Sampling Gap is geographic rather than analytical. The test is not a machine; it is a survey. The access problem is not a museum curator but the ocean itself, the sand, the forest canopy. He cannot dive every continental shelf personally, though he has done more than two hundred dives at a single site off the coast of Japan. He cannot excavate the Sahara. He can only point at the unsearched territory and say: you have not looked there.

This is a weaker form of the Sampling Gap than Sklyarov’s, because Hancock’s claim about what lies in the uninvestigated territory is more speculative. Sklyarov could point at a specific kerf on a specific block and say: test this. Hancock points at twenty-seven million square kilometers of ocean floor and says: look here. The first is actionable; the second is a research program that would take generations and cost billions.

But the structural feature is the same. Both researchers identified tests that would advance the argument. Both found the tests blocked by factors outside their control. And both were then criticized for the untestability of claims that were, in fact, testable but untested.

Let me tell you about one more case, because it shows the Sampling Gap at its sharpest.

In approximately 2012, on an expedition to Japan, Sklyarov’s team examined a stone feature called Masuda-iwafun, a massive carved granite monolith near the town of Asuka. The object is roughly eleven feet long, eight feet wide, and five feet tall, with a complex geometry that includes two rectangular basins and a network of channels. Its purpose is unknown; conventional explanations range from a sake press to a funeral monument. Sklyarov, characteristically, was less interested in its purpose than in how it was made. The cuts on its surface showed the same anomalous precision he had documented at sites in Egypt, Peru, and Turkey.

His team took surface samples. This was not officially sanctioned. The account in the book describes a process that would be familiar to any field scientist who has ever worked in a country where the bureaucracy of permissions exceeds the bureaucracy of actual research: you collect what you can, you do it carefully, and you publish whatever you find.

The samples were analyzed by scanning electron microscope. In the surface residue, the analysis found a particle of Cu-Fe-Ni-Co alloy. Copper-iron-nickel-cobalt. This is a high-technology industrial alloy. It does not appear in any documented period of Japanese metallurgy. It does not appear in any documented period of any pre-modern metallurgy anywhere on Earth. It appears in modern cutting tools, specifically in the metal-matrix composites used for stone-cutting blades in industrial applications.

Sklyarov labeled these results “preliminary.” He noted that full analysis was pending. He noted that additional samples from the same site would be needed, and that comparison with samples from other sites showing similar marks would be necessary to rule out contamination or coincidence. He called for wavelength-dispersive X-ray analysis, a more precise technique than the standard energy-dispersive method.

The full analysis was never completed. The Laboratory of Alternative History continued to operate, but the institutional capacity to conduct follow-up laboratory work was limited and diminished over time. The preliminary result from Masuda-iwafun sits in the published record, in a Russian-language book that has been read by a few hundred enthusiasts, as an unverified data point of potentially extraordinary significance.

Think about what this means. A scanning electron microscope found, on the surface of an ancient carved granite monolith in Japan, a particle of an alloy that matches modern industrial stone-cutting compounds and matches no known ancient metallurgy. The finding has not been replicated, has not been peer-reviewed, has not been subjected to the ordinary processes by which a scientific result becomes established. It exists in a kind of evidentiary limbo: too specific to ignore, too unverified to cite, too institutionally orphaned to ever be followed up by the kind of laboratory that could either confirm or bury it.

This is the Sampling Gap in its purest form. Not a claim that cannot be tested. A test result that cannot be completed.

I want to be careful about what the Sampling Gap does and does not prove.

It does not prove that Sklyarov was right about extraterrestrial builders. It does not prove that Hancock is right about a lost civilization. It does not prove that the saw kerfs at Giza were made by diamond blades, or that the Saqqara vessels were turned on alien lathes, or that the stone on Easter Island was temporarily softened by a process unknown to modern chemistry.

What the Sampling Gap proves is narrower, and in some ways more uncomfortable. It proves that the most direct tests of the most specific claims made by both researchers have not been performed. It proves that the barrier to those tests is institutional, not technical. And it proves that the mainstream critique of unfalsifiability, while legitimate in some cases, is inapplicable in others, because the claims in question are falsifiable and the falsification has simply not been attempted.

This puts the mainstream in an awkward position. If you do not perform the test, you cannot say the hypothesis has been tested. If you cannot say the hypothesis has been tested, you cannot say it has failed. And if you cannot say it has failed, then the confidence with which you dismiss it rests on something other than evidence. It rests on judgment, on prior probability, on the assessment that the hypothesis is so unlikely that the test is not worth performing. This may be a reasonable assessment. Many hypotheses are so unlikely that testing them is a poor use of limited resources. But it is an assessment, not a finding. It is a decision about what questions deserve answers, and like all such decisions, it reflects the priorities and blind spots of the people making it.

The Sampling Gap has a cousin in the history of science, and it is worth spending a moment there.

In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian physician working in the maternity ward of the Vienna General Hospital, noticed that women delivered by doctors had a mortality rate five times higher than women delivered by midwives. He traced the difference to a single variable: the doctors came to the maternity ward directly from the autopsy room, carrying cadaverous particles on their hands. He introduced a policy of hand-washing with chlorinated lime. The mortality rate dropped from roughly ten percent to roughly one percent.

The medical establishment rejected his findings. Not because the evidence was ambiguous. The mortality data was clear. Not because the experiment was poorly designed. The before-and-after comparison was as clean as any natural experiment in medicine. They rejected it because the theory behind it (that invisible particles from corpses could cause disease in living patients) was incompatible with the prevailing miasma theory of disease, and because accepting it would require every physician in Europe to admit that he had been killing his patients through negligence.

Semmelweis could not identify the particles. He called them “cadaverous particles,” not bacteria, because the germ theory of disease had not yet been established. His claim was specific (hand-washing reduces mortality) and testable (compare wards with and without hand-washing) and confirmed by his own data. But the mechanism was invisible, and the professional implications were intolerable, and so the test results were dismissed by the people who most needed to hear them.

The Sampling Gap is not identical to the Semmelweis case. Sklyarov and Hancock are not practicing physicians with patients dying around them. The stakes are professional reputations and tenure decisions, not human lives. But the structural parallel is real: in both cases, a test exists, the test has been performed (or specified), and the result is rejected or ignored not because it is wrong but because the people in a position to act on it would rather not know.

There is one more dimension to the Sampling Gap that deserves attention, and it concerns the researchers themselves.

Sklyarov was aware, throughout his career, that his laboratory evidence was his weakest methodological link. He said so explicitly. The methodology he used where laboratory analysis was unavailable was what he called “morphological argument”: if the cut looks like a disk-saw cut, if the kerf width matches a disk-saw kerf, if the overrun geometry at the end of the cut matches the arc of a rotating disk, then the inference to a disk saw is supported by morphology even without direct chemical confirmation of the blade material. This is a legitimate analytical method. Forensic investigators use it. Manufacturing engineers use it. But it is weaker than direct chemical analysis, and Sklyarov knew it was weaker.

His response to this weakness was characteristic of his personality and his training. He did not compensate by softening his claims. He compensated by being scrupulously precise about the claims’ evidential status. His books use a calibrated probability language that functions as a reliable guide to his own confidence level. “Beyond any doubt” is reserved for directly measured physical facts. “With high probability” is used for strong inferences from multiple convergent lines. “Most likely” marks his preferred interpretation where alternatives remain possible. “Presumably” marks hypotheses advanced for consideration. “Cannot be excluded” marks possibilities he considers unlikely but not refutable.

This is not the language of a believer. It is the language of a physicist who has been trained to distinguish between what the data says and what the data implies. The distinction matters, because the Sampling Gap affects these two categories differently. What the data says (the kerf has this width, the surface has this flatness, the crystal structure has this orientation) is not affected by the absence of laboratory analysis. What the data implies (the blade was made of this material, the process involved this chemistry, the tool was powered by this mechanism) is affected, because the implication depends on ruling out alternatives, and ruling out alternatives requires the kind of direct analysis that the Sampling Gap prevents.

Hancock operates differently. His evidential approach relies less on laboratory analysis and more on the convergence of multiple independent lines: geological, astronomical, mathematical, mythological. Where Sklyarov’s strength is the precision of individual measurements, Hancock’s strength is the accumulation of separate threads pointing in the same direction. This means the Sampling Gap affects Hancock less directly. He does not need a microgram of kerf residue; he needs a pattern of independent anomalies that converge. His vulnerability is different: not the absence of a test result, but the difficulty of distinguishing convergence from coincidence.

But both of them, in the end, face the same wall. Both have identified questions whose answers are locked behind doors they cannot open. And both have been criticized for the silence that greets them when they knock.

Consider an analogy closer to daily life. A building develops a crack in a load-bearing wall. A resident points it out. The building manager says: that crack is cosmetic. The resident hires a structural engineer, who says: I would like to test the concrete around that crack for carbonation depth and chloride penetration. The building manager says: no. The testing would damage the wall. The engineer says: the sample is smaller than a pencil eraser. The building manager says: we are not authorized to permit testing. The building is a landmark. The crack is cosmetic. Go home.

The resident goes home. The crack is still there. The test that would determine whether the crack is cosmetic or structural has been refused. The building is declared safe. No one explains how anyone knows.

This is the Sampling Gap. It is the gap between a question and its answer, maintained by a decision that the question is not worth asking. The decision may be correct. Many cracks are cosmetic. Many anomalies in the archaeological record have conventional explanations that are perfectly adequate. But the decision not to test is not the same as the conclusion that the test would yield a negative result. It is a bet, and like all bets, it carries risk.

The risk, in the case of the ancient stone surfaces, is that the test might produce a result that does not fit. A particle of nickel-cobalt alloy where no nickel-cobalt alloy should be. An absence of copper where copper should be abundant. A crystal structure at the surface that differs from the crystal structure a centimeter below, in a way that implies a process no textbook describes.

These are not frightening results. They are informative results. They would advance the conversation regardless of which direction they pointed. If the kerf residue is copper and sand, then the conventional explanation is strengthened and Sklyarov’s hypothesis is weakened and everyone can move on. If the residue is something else, then the conversation changes, and the change is productive, because a question has been answered.

The Sampling Gap is not about protecting archaeology from bad answers. It is about protecting archaeology from answers. And the protection is not a conspiracy. It is a system of incentives, priorities, and institutional norms that collectively produce the same result a conspiracy would produce: the question goes unasked, the test goes unperformed, and the gap between evidence and explanation remains exactly where it is.

Here is where the Sampling Gap connects to the larger story of this book.

Chapters 1 and 2 showed where Hancock and Sklyarov agree. They agree on the Tolerance Problem: the measured precision of certain ancient constructions exceeds the capabilities of the tools attributed to their credited builders. They agree on the Competence Inversion: at site after site, the oldest construction layer is the most sophisticated, and each subsequent layer is cruder. These are the physical observations that form the eighty percent.

The Sampling Gap is about what happens next. Both researchers looked at the same anomalies and proposed explanations. Hancock proposed a lost human civilization. Sklyarov proposed a non-human technological presence. Both explanations are, in principle, testable. Both researchers specified tests. Neither test has been performed.

This matters because it changes the character of the disagreement between the mainstream and the alternatives. The disagreement is not purely about interpretation, though interpretation is part of it. The disagreement is partly about access. The people who control the material have decided, through a thousand individual decisions about research priorities and permit applications and museum access policies, that the questions raised by the outsiders do not merit the resources required to answer them.

This is a reasonable institutional posture. Resources are finite. Museums cannot grant sampling access to everyone who asks. Archaeological sites cannot accommodate every researcher with a theory. The gatekeeping serves a function: it prevents the material from being consumed by inquiry at the expense of preservation. These are real concerns and they should not be dismissed.

But the gatekeeping also has a cost, and the cost is the Sampling Gap itself: a growing inventory of untested claims, accumulating over decades, that cannot be resolved by argument alone and that grow more expensive to ignore with each passing year. Because the technology for non-destructive analysis is improving. Portable X-ray fluorescence analyzers now perform elemental analysis in the field, in seconds, without removing material from the surface. Surface-adaptive micro-XRF scanners can map elemental distributions across irregular stone surfaces. Emerging analytical techniques can classify surface compositions without destroying the sample.

The tests that Sklyarov specified in 2014 were difficult then. Some of them required invasive sampling. Some of them required laboratory equipment that could not be brought to the field. The technology has changed. The access has not.

And so the Sampling Gap remains: the distance between a question and its answer, measured not in technical difficulty but in institutional willingness. It is the space where the conversation about the ancient past is frozen, not by the limits of what we can know, but by the limits of what we have chosen to ask.

Sklyarov, in the methodology section of his corpus, put it with the directness of a physicist who has spent too long explaining himself to people who do not share his training. The methodology, he wrote, was constrained to what could be analyzed from the surface without removing material. This limited it to morphological analysis and comparative photography. The gap between what the evidence implied and what available analytical methods could directly confirm was the honest central limitation of the body of work.

Honest is the important word. He did not pretend the limitation did not exist. He did not claim that morphological analysis was equivalent to chemical analysis. He did not close the Sampling Gap with rhetoric. He described it, measured it, and left it open.

This is, in a way, the most Sklyarov-like thing about Sklyarov. A man trained at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, in a system that drilled into its students the absolute primacy of measured evidence over theoretical preference, who spent twenty years accumulating photographic and morphological data that pointed toward a conclusion he could not chemically confirm, who described the limitation in the same calibrated language he used for everything else, and whose specified tests remain unperformed.

Hancock is still alive. His version of the Sampling Gap (the uninvestigated continental shelves, the unsurveyed Sahara, the unexcavated Amazon) is still open. The test, in his case, is not a spectrometer but an expedition, not a laboratory but a research vessel. He has been calling for systematic underwater archaeology of the drowned continental margins for over two decades. The call has not been answered on the scale he proposes, though individual underwater projects at specific sites have produced results that he cites as encouraging.

The Sampling Gap, for both of them, is not a failure of evidence. It is a failure of testing. And the failure of testing is not a scientific problem. It is a social one, the kind of problem that resolves itself only when the cost of not knowing exceeds the cost of finding out.

Barry Marshall drank the petri dish because the cost of not knowing, in his case, was measured in human lives: patients with ulcers receiving acid-reduction therapy instead of antibiotics, patients whose stomachs were being surgically removed for a condition curable with a two-week course of pills. The cost was legible, and it was intolerable, and so Marshall bypassed the institutional blockage by making himself the test subject.

Nobody is going to drink a petri dish for the Saqqara vessels. Nobody’s life depends on knowing what alloy cut the stone at Masuda-iwafun. The cost of not knowing, in the case of the Sampling Gap, is measured in a different currency: years of argument that could be settled, questions that could be answered, and two research programs, totaling forty books and sixty combined years of work, that remain in a state of suspense because the evidence that would resolve them is locked in stone that nobody with the right instruments has been allowed to touch.
