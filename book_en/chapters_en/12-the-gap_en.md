# Chapter 12: The Gap

On a winter afternoon in Moscow, sometime in late 2015, a man sat in an apartment surrounded by manuscripts. The manuscripts were stacked in the way that manuscripts accumulate around a person who is finishing two books simultaneously: not neatly, not in a single pile, but in concentric circles of paper radiating outward from the desk to the floor to the adjacent chair to the cleared space on the dining table where dinner used to go before the books took over.

The man was fifty-four years old. His name was Andrey Yurievich Sklyarov. He had been a physicist, a political organizer, a television presenter, and for the last nineteen years of his life, an investigator of ancient stone. He had led expeditions to twelve countries. He had authored thirty-two works examining archaeological anomalies that mainstream science had declined to engage with. He was completing his final two manuscripts: Creation of Ancient Civilizations and Yahweh vs. Baal: Chronicle of a Coup. Both would be published in 2016.

He did not know they were his final manuscripts.

There is no public account of Sklyarov’s diagnosis, if he had one at that point. The details of his illness are not part of the public record in the way that a Western public figure’s medical history sometimes becomes public. What is known is the end: he died on September 15, 2016, at the age of fifty-five. The Laboratory of Alternative History, the organization he founded, continued to maintain its website, where the complete corpus of his thirty-two works remained available in Russian. No obituary appeared in any Western publication. No mainstream archaeological journal noted his passing. In the English-speaking world, the event registered as barely a ripple, because in the English-speaking world, Andrey Sklyarov had never quite existed.

Consider the accumulated evidence. Photographs from Egypt, Peru, Bolivia, Mexico, Turkey, Syria, Ethiopia, Easter Island, Japan. Measurements of saw kerfs and drill grooves. Architect’s notes on construction tolerances. A preliminary SEM analysis from Japan showing a trace of nickel-cobalt alloy in a tool mark on basalt. Thirty-two books arranged around a single question, each one a different attempt to corner the answer.

The answer did not come. The tests that would have produced the answer were never performed. The samples were never collected. The laboratories he would have needed were never engaged. The permitting authorities who controlled access to the stones never opened the gate. He died with his central hypothesis exactly where it had been when he first proposed it: stated, specified, testable, untested.

Eleven chapters have circled a thesis. It is time to land it.

The divergence is familiar by now. Nineteen points where the measurements agree and the explanations split. The journalist sees human builders. The physicist sees non-human engineers. Both pass through the same evidence and emerge on opposite sides.

These two explanations cannot both be true. One of them, at minimum, is wrong. Possibly both. The standard response, the response of mainstream archaeology and of casual observers alike, is to treat this divergence as proof that the entire enterprise is unreliable. If two people look at the same data and reach opposite conclusions, something is wrong with the data, or with the people, or with both.

But there is a different reading.

The divergence is not a sign of failure. The divergence is the most informative data in the entire corpus. It is a map, and the map shows you something that no amount of agreement could ever show. It shows you where the data ends.

This is The Gap.

The Gap is the space between evidence and explanation. It exists in every field, every investigation, every life. It is the place where the data runs out and the human being continues. Most of the time, we do not notice it. When the record is strong enough, it constrains so tightly that the explanation is forced. Two plus two equals four, and there is no gap for a person to step through. But when the record loosens its grip, when the data points to multiple possible conclusions, when the measurements are precise but the meaning is underdetermined, the gap opens. And what fills it is not method. It is not logic. It is not the objective application of scientific reasoning to neutral facts.

What fills it is the person.

This is not a criticism. It is not a lament. It is a description of something that happens in every domain of human inquiry where the evidence is incomplete, which is to say, in every domain of human inquiry that matters. Medicine, criminal justice, climate science, economics, psychology, history: every field where serious people must decide what incomplete evidence means produces the same phenomenon. The evidence constrains up to a point, and then the person takes over. The doctor who has seen patients die makes a different decision than the doctor who has seen patients recover, even when the chart looks the same. The detective who grew up in the neighborhood reads the crime scene differently than the detective who did not. The economist trained in Chicago applies different priors than the economist trained in Cambridge. The evidence is the same. The person is different. The conclusion diverges.

The Hancock-Sklyarov divergence is a case study in this universal phenomenon, but it is a case study of unusual clarity, because the variables are unusually well controlled. Same evidence. Same anomalies. Same measurements. Same sites. No communication between the investigators. No social influence. No possibility of groupthink. Two independent investigations, converging on the data and diverging on the interpretation. If you wanted to design an experiment to test where evidence ends and personality begins, you could not do better than this.

And the result of the experiment is legible. It is legible because the two people who stepped into the gap are so different that their footprints are unmistakable.

Consider who Graham Hancock is, at the deepest level that the biographical record can reach.

He is a journalist. He was trained at The Economist and the Sunday Times, institutions that prize the well-constructed narrative, the telling detail, the human angle. He left institutional journalism in the late 1980s and spent three decades building a case for a lost human civilization. The emphasis falls on both words: human and civilization. In Hancock’s framework, the builders of the ancient monuments were people. Extraordinary people, possessed of knowledge we have lost, but people. The gods of mythology are cultural memories of human teachers who arrived after a catastrophe and shared what they knew. The story is one of human capacity, human loss, and human recovery.

He is a man whose encounter with ayahuasca, described in Chapter 7, redirected his career around the conviction that consciousness is non-physical and that ancient shamanic traditions encode genuine knowledge about its nature.

He is a man who tells stories about human beings doing remarkable things. This is what journalists do. This is what he does. And when the evidence at an ancient site runs out, when the measurements are recorded and the anomalies documented and the question “who built this?” hangs in the air without a definitive answer, he fills the gap with what he knows. He fills it with people. Brilliant, lost, tragic, heroic people.

Now consider who Andrey Sklyarov was.

He was a physicist, trained at the Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, one of the most demanding scientific programs the Soviet Union ever produced. He held a Candidate of Physical and Mathematical Sciences degree, the rough equivalent of a Western Ph.D. His intellectual formation was rigorously materialist: the world is matter, matter obeys laws, laws are discoverable through measurement, and if something cannot be measured, it is not a proper subject for investigation.

He was a man who, across thirty-two books, never once mentioned psychedelics, altered states of consciousness, or shamanic experience. Not as data, not as methodology, not as a subject of curiosity. The entire domain of consciousness exploration that defined the second half of Hancock’s career was, for Sklyarov, simply absent. Within his framework, learning by ingesting a plant extract would have been a category error. You learn things by measuring them.

He was a man who measured things. At every site, at every opportunity, with every instrument available to him: tape measures, straightedges, feeler gauges, an architect’s surveying equipment, scanning electron microscopes when he could get access to them. He brought geologists to identify rock types, architects to document tolerances, physicists to calculate the force requirements of observed tool marks. When the measurements were complete and the anomalies documented and the question “who built this?” hung in the air, he filled the gap with what he knew. He filled it with engineering. Machines. Non-human operators who possessed technologies that could be specified even if they could not be found. The gods were not metaphors and not memories. They were engineers.

The journalist, when the record runs out, sees people. The physicist, when the record runs out, sees machines. This is not because one is right and the other is wrong. It is because the gap is exactly wide enough to let a whole person through, and each person carries their own shape.

The observation deserves precision.

The eighty percent convergence between Hancock and Sklyarov is where the evidence constrains tightly enough that personality does not matter. Both accept that the oldest construction layers at megalithic sites are the most technically sophisticated. Both accept that construction tolerances at Giza, Sacsayhuaman, Baalbek, and Ollantaytambo exceed what copper tools and stone pounders can achieve. Both accept that a global catastrophe destroyed an advanced civilization before recorded history. Both accept that the engineering measurements have not received adequate institutional attention. Both accept that flood myths encode real geological events. On all of these points, the evidence pushes both investigators to the same place, regardless of who they are.

The twenty percent divergence is where the data loosens its grip. The measurements are done. The anomalies are documented. Now: who held the tool? The tool marks are in the stone. The alloy residue (if confirmed) is in the kerf. The construction tolerances are what they are. But the identity of the builder is not in the stone. It is in the inference. And the inference, at every single point of divergence, tracks back to the person making it.

Hancock sees an advanced human civilization because he is constitutionally incapable of looking at a great work and seeing anything other than human greatness behind it. This is not a flaw. It is a lens, ground by decades of experience, and it produces a specific focal point: if something astonishing was accomplished, then astonishing people accomplished it.

Sklyarov sees a non-human civilization because he is constitutionally incapable of accepting an explanation that requires capabilities for which no physical evidence of the production infrastructure exists. No foundries, no machine shops, no tool waste, no domestic refuse. A physicist asks: where is the manufacturing base? And when the answer is “there is none,” the physicist concludes that the manufacturing base was not on this planet. This is not a flaw either. It is the same lens applied to a different surface: if the evidence for a terrestrial production base does not exist, then the production base was not terrestrial. The inference follows the logic. The logic follows the training. The training follows the person.

The gap between evidence and explanation is where the person lives. This is the thesis of this book. It has been implicit in every chapter. It is explicit here, now, for the first and last time.

The divergence between Graham Hancock and Andrey Sklyarov is not a failure of methodology. It is not a failure of rigor. It is not proof that both researchers are wrong, or that the anomalies they documented are illusory, or that the enterprise of alternative archaeology is intellectually bankrupt. It is a portrait. A portrait of two minds encountering the same silence in the record and filling it with everything they are.

The British journalist who sat with shamans and believed in the extraordinary capacities of the human spirit fills the gap with human teachers.

The Russian physicist who measured everything and trusted nothing he could not quantify fills the gap with non-human engineers.

The gap does not care which one is right. The gap is not a question. The gap is a mirror.

Here is what sits at the bottom of both careers.

At the bottom of both careers, beneath the disagreements about who built the walls and how the myths should be read and what the alloy in the kerf means and whether the gods were human or not, there is something shared that is more important than any of the specific claims.

Both men encountered a silence in the evidence. Both refused to accept that the silence meant there was nothing to hear. Both spent their professional lives filling that silence with the most rigorous work they could produce. The work was different because they were different. The rigor was real in both cases, calibrated to different standards, operating within different frameworks, producing different conclusions from the same data. And both paid for it. Hancock paid in reputation: decades of being called a crank, a pseudoscientist, a conspiracy theorist, by people who had not read his books and had not visited the sites and had not reckoned with the eighty percent convergence that his work shares with a physicist they had never heard of. Sklyarov paid in obscurity: thirty-two books in Russian, no translations, no Western audience, no Netflix series, no Joe Rogan appearances, just the quiet accumulation of evidence in a language that the Anglophone world never engaged with.

Both paid the price willingly. This is the thing about people who step into the gap. They do not do it because the gap rewards them. The gap does not reward anyone. The gap is empty. It is, by definition, the place where the evidence gives you nothing and you must give yourself. The reward, if there is one, is the work itself: the morning at the site, the measurement, the photograph, the late night at the desk with the manuscript, the argument that takes shape slowly over years, the rare moment when a pattern clicks into place and the silence, for an instant, resolves into something that almost sounds like an answer.

Almost. Because the answer, the real answer, the one that would convert the hypothesis into a finding and the finding into a fact, requires the test. And the test was never performed.

Consider the tests themselves. Not in the abstract, as institutional critique, but as specific, concrete, executable protocols that a specific man specified and that remain, as of this writing, unexecuted.

The first test concerns tool-mark residues. At sites across Egypt, Peru, Bolivia, Turkey, Syria, and Japan, Sklyarov’s expeditions documented saw cuts in granite and basalt with consistent kerf widths and arc radii matching rotating disk blades of specific diameters. The preliminary SEM finding from Masuda-Iwafune, described in Chapter 3, identified a copper-iron-nickel-cobalt particle matching no known ancient or modern conventional tool alloy. Sklyarov specified the follow-up: wavelength-dispersive X-ray analysis of additional samples from the same site and from corresponding marks at other sites, with full spectral data published against a comprehensive reference database of known alloy compositions.

The second test concerns the stone-softening phenomenon. At sites in Peru, Bolivia, Easter Island, and Egypt, Sklyarov documented what he called “plasticine technology”: stone surfaces (granite, basalt, limestone, calcite) that appeared to have been brought to a temporarily plastic state, shaped by hand or simple implements, and then re-hardened. The evidence included crossing groove lines at the same plane level (impossible with a rigid stone), lateral material displacement at groove edges, and what he described as finger-trace impressions in stone that is currently as hard as any stone of its type. He specified the test: chemical analysis of the treated stone surfaces to identify any alteration layer, residual reagent, or structural change at the boundary between treated and untreated stone. He suggested examining the crystal structure (crystalline versus amorphous) at the surface to determine whether thermal or chemical modification had occurred.

The third test concerns genetic hybridization. Sklyarov argued that modern Homo sapiens was created by genetic modification of archaic Homo sapiens by his proposed extraterrestrial civilization. He made a specific, falsifiable prediction: the human genome contains genetic sequences not attributable to known primate lineages, sequences that appear inserted rather than evolved. He acknowledged that no one had looked: “So far no traces of such a gene pool seem to have been found, but nobody has been looking for them.” He proposed the search strategy: full ancient DNA comparison of archaic Homo sapiens specimens against modern human genomes, specifically looking for non-primate insertions.

Each of these tests is specific. Each is executable with existing technology. Each would produce results that would either support or refute a central claim in Sklyarov’s framework. Each has been specified in print, in detail, by the man who made the claim. Not one has been performed.

The tests matter because they occupy a unique position in the epistemology of this entire field.

Both Hancock and Sklyarov have been criticized for making unfalsifiable claims. The criticism has some justice when applied to certain elements of their frameworks: Hancock’s non-interference hypothesis is, by his own description, unfalsifiable in its current form. Sklyarov’s treatment of mythology applies no consistent principle for distinguishing reliable from unreliable content.

But the physical claims are not unfalsifiable. As Chapters 3 and 11 established, they are testable, specified, and unperformed.

This book does not contain the resolution. The resolution would require one of three things: an institutional decision to permit the tests, a technological workaround that renders institutional permission unnecessary (portable non-contact spectroscopy is approaching this threshold), or a researcher with the credentials, the funding, and the institutional independence to run the tests without asking for permission.

But the stakes can be named.

If the tests are run, and the results confirm conventional construction methods (copper tools, stone pounders, human labor, no anomalous alloys, no plasticine technology, no non-primate genetic insertions), then both alternative frameworks collapse simultaneously. The eighty percent convergence between Hancock and Sklyarov becomes a shared error, and the twenty percent divergence becomes an argument between two people who were both wrong about the same thing for different reasons. This would be a result. It would be a valuable result. It would close the gap, or at least narrow it substantially.

If the tests are run, and the results reveal anomalous residues, tool signatures, or material compositions that are inconsistent with the tools and technologies attributed to the credited builders, then both alternative frameworks are partially vindicated. The anomaly is real. The evidence supports what both men documented with photographs and measurements and preliminary analyses over decades. But the question of the agent, the question of who held the tool, remains open. The alloy in the kerf does not tell you whether the hand that guided it was human or not. It tells you only that the tool existed and that no one has explained it.

Either result is worth having. Either result advances knowledge. Either result is better than what we have now, which is nothing: no tests, no results, no samples collected, no questions answered, no gap narrowed.

And one of the two people who identified the questions is dead.

The cause of death is not publicly known. Russian-language sources accessible through the Laboratory of Alternative History website and associated forums do not specify a diagnosis. What the record shows is an active researcher who published two books in his final year, whose works represent the culmination of a nineteen-year intellectual arc from desk-bound theorist to field investigator to synthetic thinker.

There is something about the relationship between a person and a body of work that resists expression in any other form.

Thirty-two books. Twelve countries. Thousands of photographs. Hundreds of measurements. An architect’s surveys, a geologist’s rock identifications, an SEM analysis with a preliminary result that no one ever followed up on. A physicist’s meticulous insistence on asking “what technology is physically required to produce this feature?” before asking “who possessed that technology?” A calibrated probability language, applied consistently across two decades: “beyond any doubt” for directly measured physical facts, “with high probability” for strong inferences, “most likely” for preferred interpretations, “speculatively” for hypotheses being advanced for consideration. A career built on the proposition that the evidence matters more than the explanation, that measurement precedes interpretation, that the stone speaks if you listen with the right instruments.

And then: the instruments were never applied. The stone was never asked the question in the only language it could answer. The test was never run.

There is a word for spending your life preparing a question that never gets asked. The word is not “failure.” Failure implies that the outcome was within the questioner’s control. Sklyarov did everything within his control. He identified the sites. He documented the anomalies. He specified the tests. He offered to fund the work. The part that was not within his control was the part that mattered: the permission. The access. The institutional willingness to let the question be asked in a form that could produce an answer.

The answer never came. The question remains.

Graham Hancock is seventy-five years old. He is still writing. Still arguing. Still filling the gap with his particular vision of lost human greatness. In 2024, he sat across from an academic archaeologist on Joe Rogan’s podcast and debated for four hours. He conceded points. He was pressed on claims he had made in earlier books and acknowledged that some of them no longer held. He said, of his Netflix series: “I was perhaps over-vigorous.” He reported a reconciliation dinner with Zahi Hawass, the former secretary-general of the Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities, with whom he had feuded for years. He described his lost civilization as “a black hole in space to me,” a thing he believed in but could not see, defined by its effects rather than its substance.

This is the behavior of a man still in the gap. Still filling it. Still revising the fill as new evidence and new challenges arrive. The gap has not closed around him any more than it closed around Sklyarov. The evidence remains incomplete. The tests remain unperformed (different tests, in Hancock’s case: systematic archaeological investigation of the submerged continental shelves, professional excavation of the underwater sites he has identified, the kind of large-scale survey that LiDAR and modern remote sensing are only now making possible). The question remains open.

But Hancock has time. He is still alive. His books are in English, which means they are accessible to the global scientific community. His audience is large enough that the institutional response to his work, however dismissive, is at least a response. He has been engaged with, argued against, debated in public forums. The refusal, in his case, is not total. It is partial: the tests have not been run, but the conversation has not been silenced.

Sklyarov had no such luxury. Thirty-two books in Russian. No translations. No Western audience. No public debate with a mainstream archaeologist, because no mainstream archaeologist in the English-speaking world knew he existed. The refusal, in his case, was total, and it was total not through any act of suppression but through the simple, devastating fact that none of his works had ever been published in English. His evidence was invisible not because it was hidden but because it existed in a language that the Anglophone scientific establishment never engaged with.

His findings remain unread by the community best positioned to test them.

The gap between evidence and explanation is where the person lives. Twelve chapters have shown what that looks like in practice: two researchers, two frameworks, two different shapes pressed into the same silence. The convergence (eighty percent, the space where the evidence is strong enough that personality does not matter) and the divergence (twenty percent, the space where the evidence loosens its grip and the person steps through). The journalist fills the gap with people. The physicist fills it with machines. The institutional architecture prevents the gap from being narrowed by the tests that would narrow it.

One more thing, and then a question.

The gap is not unique to archaeology. It is not unique to alternative research. It is not a special condition afflicting the fringe. The gap is the human condition. It exists wherever the evidence is incomplete, which is everywhere. It exists in the doctor’s office, when the scan is ambiguous and the physician must decide whether to operate. It exists in the courtroom, when the evidence is circumstantial and the jury must decide what it means. It exists in the climate model, when the parameters are uncertain and the modeler must decide which scenarios to present. It exists in every relationship, every career decision, every moment when you must act on less information than you would like and fill the difference with something that comes from you, not from the data.

The question is always the same: what do you fill it with?

Most people fill the gap with convention. They do what has been done before, believe what has been believed before, conclude what has been concluded before. This is not cowardice. It is efficiency. Consider the Egyptologist at Cairo University, teaching the standard chronology semester after semester, who has never visited the Serapeum with a straightedge. He is not hiding evidence. He is doing his job. Convention exists because it works most of the time, and the cost of challenging convention is high, and most people have other things to do with their lives than spend them testing the foundations of knowledge they will never personally need to apply.

Some people fill the gap with fear. They see the uncertainty and retreat. They close the question by refusing to ask it. This is not cowardice either. It is self-preservation. The gap is frightening because it has no floor. You can fall into a question about who built the pyramids and never come back. You can spend thirty years and forty books and your health and your reputation and your professional standing, and at the end the gap is still there, still open, still unanswered, still hungry.

And a very few people fill the gap with themselves. They walk into the silence with whatever they have, whatever training and temperament and biographical accident have equipped them with, and they build something in there. Not an answer, necessarily. Not a proof. Something more provisional and more honest: a framework, a body of work, a life’s worth of measurements and arguments and revisions that together constitute the most serious response they can make to the silence.

Hancock did this. Sklyarov did this. They did it differently, because they are different people. The difference is the finding.

Here is the question.

Sklyarov’s central physical claim is that tool-mark residues at ancient sites on multiple continents contain an anomalous nickel-cobalt alloy not found in any known ancient metallurgical tradition. He reported preliminary SEM confirmation from one site. He specified the follow-up protocols. He identified the laboratories and instruments required. He offered to fund the work.

The tests have not been performed. He has been dead for ten years. The stone surfaces he documented are still there, in Egypt, Peru, Turkey, Japan, Syria (though some Syrian sites have been damaged or destroyed by war, and the surfaces that existed when Sklyarov examined them may no longer survive). The instruments required to analyze those surfaces without touching them, without damaging them, without requiring institutional permission to collect a sample, now fit in a handheld device.

What would happen if someone ran the tests?

No one knows the answer. That is the point. That is the gap. The gap between evidence and explanation, the gap that Hancock has filled with lost human teachers and Sklyarov filled with extraterrestrial engineers, is not a permanent feature of the universe. It is a contingent feature of our ignorance. It can be narrowed. It can, in principle, be closed. The tools exist. The protocols exist. The sites exist. The question has been asked, by a man who is no longer alive to hear the answer.

This is what the book leaves you with. Not a conclusion. Not a verdict on who built the ancient walls or whether the gods were human or not. A question. The simplest possible question, the question that both men asked for decades, the question that the institutional structure of modern archaeology has, through a thousand individually reasonable decisions, prevented from being answered.

The gap does not care who fills it. The gap does not care whether you are a journalist or a physicist, British or Russian, famous or obscure. The gap is indifferent to credentials, to languages, to the length of your bibliography. The gap is the place where evidence stops and you begin.

Andrey Sklyarov filled the gap with everything he had for nineteen years. He filled it with measurements and photographs and field expeditions and thirty-two books in a language that the Anglophone world never translated. He filled it with the particular shape of a physicist who trusted measurement above all other forms of knowledge and who followed the measurements to a conclusion that cost him any chance of mainstream acceptance. He filled the gap with who he was. He died at fifty-five, with the gap still open.

The tests he specified would take weeks, not years. The instruments exist. The sites exist. The question exists. The person who asked it does not.

What would happen if someone ran the tests?
