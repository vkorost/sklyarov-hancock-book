# Chapter 11: The Refusal

Barry Marshall, whose story was told in Chapter 3, broke through by swallowing the bacterium himself, because no institutional pathway would let him test the hypothesis on anyone else. He had data. He had published it. The medical establishment did not dispute it. They ignored it. For nearly a decade, the finding sat in the published record, reproducible and specific, while millions of patients continued to receive treatment for a disease model that was wrong.

Not refuted. Ignored.

This is a distinction worth pausing on, because it describes a phenomenon more common in science than scientists typically admit. A finding can be challenged, and the challenge is a form of engagement. A finding can be replicated, and the replication is a form of validation. A finding can be shown to be wrong, and the demonstration is a form of progress. All of these are scientific responses. But there is a fourth response, one that falls outside the boundaries of the scientific method entirely: the finding can be declined. Not tested. Not challenged. Not replicated. Simply set aside, with the implicit understanding that the question it raises is not worth asking.

This phenomenon needs a name. Call it The Refusal.

The Refusal is not a conspiracy. It does not require secret meetings, coordinated suppression, or institutional malice. It requires only three things that are individually reasonable and collectively devastating: a gatekeeping structure that controls access to evidence, a professional incentive system that rewards confirmation of the existing framework, and a shared assumption among gatekeepers that certain questions are beneath the threshold of seriousness. Each of these things, taken alone, is defensible. A limited archaeological resource requires access controls. A professional community requires standards. A scientific field requires some mechanism for distinguishing productive questions from unproductive ones. The problem is not that any single element of the system is irrational. The problem is that the system, as a whole, produces a specific outcome: certain questions do not get asked. Certain tests do not get run. Certain evidence does not get examined. And the non-examination is then cited as proof that the evidence does not exist.

Marshall understood this. His self-experiment in 1984 was not a stunt. It was the only path through the institutional blockade. The NIH did not formally recognize H. pylori as the primary cause of peptic ulcers until 1994, a full decade later. The Nobel Prize came in 2005. The delay was caused by The Refusal: the systemic, decentralized, individually rational decision by the people who controlled the gates that the question was not worth engaging with.

The Refusal is not a claim that gatekeepers are stupid or corrupt. The gastroenterologists who dismissed Marshall were brilliant researchers whose resistance was, from within their framework, entirely rational. The pharmaceutical companies were operating within an established market structure that had no incentive to fund a two-week cure for a condition generating lifelong revenue. None of it required dishonesty. It required only that no one ask an inconvenient question.

The Refusal is what happens when the filter stops discriminating between noise and signal and begins filtering on the basis of source rather than content. When the question is no longer “is this evidence valid?” but “is this person credentialed enough to ask?”

In 2004, Sklyarov arrived in Egypt with cameras, measuring equipment, and an interdisciplinary team (described more fully in Chapter 9). They wanted to look at the sarcophagi in the Serapeum at Saqqara.

The Serapeum is an underground complex, discovered in 1851 by Auguste Mariette, containing twenty-four enormous granite and basalt boxes. Each box weighs approximately sixty to seventy tons. The lids weigh an additional twenty to thirty tons. The boxes are carved from single blocks of stone, and the interior surfaces are polished to a mirror-like finish. The official attribution is to the Twenty-sixth Dynasty of Egypt, approximately 664 to 525 BCE, though some boxes have been tentatively associated with earlier periods.

Sklyarov’s team documented the sarcophagi. They measured tolerances. They photographed surface finishes. They noted the same quality stratification described in Chapter 2: the finest work at the deepest, oldest layers. Their measurements showed the tolerances described in Chapter 1, construction quality that could not be reconciled with the tools attributed to the credited builders.

What they did not do was take samples.

They could not take samples. The Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities (now the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities) controls access to every major archaeological site in Egypt. Research permits are issued through a formal application process that requires institutional affiliation, a detailed research plan, and approval from the Council’s permanent committee. The process is designed for affiliated academics from recognized universities and research institutions. Sklyarov's organization, the Laboratory of Alternative History, had no standing in the Egyptian (or any other) archaeological permitting system.

He had specified the tests. In his books, he described exactly what needed to be done: chemical analysis of surface residues in the saw kerfs that appeared on multiple blocks at multiple Egyptian sites, looking for compounds associated with advanced cutting materials. Microscopic examination of crystal structure at worked stone surfaces, looking for evidence of thermal or chemical modification that would indicate the use of technologies beyond copper saws and stone pounders. Spectral analysis of the anomalous nickel-cobalt alloy residue that preliminary testing had identified in tool marks at sites in Egypt, Peru, and Japan.

These were not vague requests. They were specific protocols, stated in the language of materials science, that any equipped laboratory could perform. Sklyarov was a physicist. He knew what instruments were required. He knew what results would confirm or refute his claims. And he could not perform the tests because the people who controlled access to the stones had no institutional mechanism for granting access to a self-funded Russian physicist whose conclusions might complicate the existing narrative.

Fairness to the gatekeepers is the only way to see the problem clearly.

The Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities has responsibilities that extend far beyond the facilitation of research. It oversees a national heritage that generates billions of dollars in tourism revenue. It manages sites that are physically deteriorating under the pressure of millions of annual visitors. It must balance the scientific interest in studying the monuments against the commercial interest in keeping them open to tourists and the conservation interest in preventing further damage. Every sample taken from a stone surface, however small, is a piece of an irreplaceable artifact that will never be restored. Every research expedition that enters a site consumes time, creates wear, and imposes logistical costs. The permitting system exists because the alternative, unregulated access, would be catastrophic.

The gastroenterologists who dismissed Marshall’s H. pylori hypothesis also had reasons. The acid-secretion model of ulcer disease was not a fantasy. Excess acid does damage the stomach lining. Stress does affect gastrointestinal function. The existing treatment regime, while not curative, did reduce symptoms in most patients. A responsible physician, confronted with a claim that contradicted established understanding, has a professional obligation to demand rigorous evidence before changing treatment protocols. The standard of evidence in medicine is high for a reason: people can die if you get it wrong.

The reasons, in both cases, are real. And in both cases, the reasons produce the same outcome: the test does not get run. The question does not get asked. And the absence of the test is then cited as evidence that the claim is unfounded. “There is no evidence that H. pylori causes ulcers” and “there is no data showing that advanced tool technologies were used at Giza” are statements that are true in the narrow sense and misleading in the broader one. There is no data because no one has looked. And no one has looked because the looking would require the cooperation of the people who have the most to lose from the finding.

This is the structure of The Refusal. Not a wall. A filter. The filter is made of individually reasonable decisions, and it produces a collectively unreasonable outcome: the material that would settle the question does not get collected.

The Refusal operates differently in archaeology than it does in medicine, and the difference is worth understanding, because it explains why the alternative archaeology community’s grievance, however overwrought it sometimes sounds, has a structural basis that cannot be dismissed as paranoia.

In medicine, the material is, in principle, available to anyone. A bacterium can be cultured in any laboratory. A clinical trial can be run at any hospital. The gatekeeping in medicine is social (who gets funded, who gets published, who gets invited to conferences) but not physical. Marshall could culture H. pylori in his own lab. He could infect himself. The samples were accessible even when the institutions were hostile.

In archaeology, the record is physical and site-specific. The stones are in Egypt, Peru, Turkey, Japan, Lebanon, and a dozen other countries. You cannot culture a megalithic wall in a laboratory. You cannot run a clinical trial on a sarcophagus. The evidence lives at the site, and access to the site is controlled by national governments, international conventions (UNESCO World Heritage designations impose additional restrictions), and local archaeological authorities. The permitting process is not merely social. It is legal. An unauthorized sample collection at the Giza Plateau is not a career risk. It is a criminal act.

This creates a situation that is structurally unique to archaeology among the physical sciences. The material that would test the alternative claims exists in a specific physical location, and access to that location is controlled by an institutional structure that has no incentive to facilitate tests whose results might complicate the established framework. The alternative researchers have identified the tests. They have specified the protocols. In some cases, they have offered to fund the work. The answer, with rare exceptions, has been no.

The specifics matter more than the generalization.

Sklyarov documented tool marks at multiple sites in Egypt (2004 and subsequent expeditions), Peru and Bolivia (2007), Mexico (2007 and 2008), Turkey and Syria (2009 and 2012), Ethiopia (2008), Japan (2013), and Easter Island (2013). At nearly every site, his team was restricted to photographing and visually measuring from permitted tourist paths. The Easter Island confrontation described in Chapter 3, where a team member stepped off the path to photograph a saw-cut mark on a pukao, produced a photograph. It did not produce a sample.

At the Masuda-Iwafune site in Japan, described in Chapter 3, his team obtained the one surface sample that produced a preliminary SEM result. The full analysis was never completed.

Schoch, whose work was described in Chapter 10, has publicly requested permission to conduct geological sampling of the Sphinx enclosure walls. The sampling would be minimally invasive: small core samples from the bedrock, analyzed for weathering patterns that would definitively establish whether the erosion was caused by precipitation or by salt crystallization. The samples would provide data that both sides of the debate agree would be conclusive. The Egyptian authorities have not granted the request. The request has been outstanding for over three decades.

Graham Hancock has argued for decades that the submerged continental shelves of the world’s oceans contain undiscovered evidence of a pre-catastrophe civilization. He has identified specific sites (off the coast of Japan, India, Malta, the Bahamas) where he has personally dived and documented formations that he believes are man-made. He has publicly challenged mainstream archaeology to investigate these sites with professional resources: multibeam sonar, side-scan sonar, remotely operated vehicles, and systematic excavation. The challenge has not been taken up. The approximately twenty-seven million square kilometers of continental shelf that were above water during the last Ice Age and are now submerged remain less than five percent surveyed by archaeological methods.

Hancock’s complaint about the non-investigation of underwater sites is not that archaeologists have investigated and found nothing. It is that they have not investigated at all. The absence of findings is the product of the absence of investigation, not the absence of findings.

There is a pattern here, and the pattern is the point.

In every case I have described, the following sequence occurs. An outsider researcher identifies a testable claim. The outsider specifies the test. The test requires physical access to material controlled by an institutional gatekeeper. The gatekeeper declines to provide access or to perform the test. The outsider’s claim is then characterized as “unscientific” because it lacks the test results that would make it scientific.

The circularity is perfect. The claim cannot be tested because the test is not permitted. The claim is dismissed because it has not been tested. The dismissal justifies the continued non-permission. The loop closes.

This circularity is not intentional. The Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities has never convened a meeting to discuss how to suppress the claims of alternative researchers. The UNESCO World Heritage Committee does not consider, among its mandate to protect cultural heritage, the secondary mandate of protecting the interpretive framework that cultural heritage departments have built around the sites they manage. No individual gatekeeper wakes up in the morning and thinks: today I will prevent the collection of evidence that might complicate our understanding of the past.

The reality is simpler and, in its way, more troubling. The system operates exactly as designed. Every individual decision within it is rational. And the aggregate outcome of all those rational decisions is the systematic non-collection of data that would be relevant to questions the system has classified as not worth asking.

This is not malice. It is architecture.

The architecture has several features that are worth examining individually.

The first is the permitting structure. In Egypt, all archaeological research requires a permit from the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities. The permit application must specify the research question, the methodology, the institutional affiliation of the applicant, and the qualifications of the research team. The Ministry’s permanent committee reviews applications and decides which projects align with national research priorities. This is a standard structure, common to every country with significant archaeological resources. It exists because the alternative (unregulated digging) would be destructive and is, in fact, criminal in most jurisdictions.

But the permitting structure has a consequence that is rarely discussed: it gives the permitting authority the ability to determine which questions get asked. A permit for a project titled “Non-destructive spectral analysis of tool-mark residues on Old Kingdom granite surfaces” would, if submitted by an unaffiliated alternative researcher, face obstacles that the same project, submitted by a professor at the University of Cairo, would not. The obstacle is not corruption. It is categorization. The permitting authority must decide, among many applications, which projects to approve with limited access windows and staff resources. An application from an institution they recognize, asking a question they consider legitimate, using a methodology they understand, will be evaluated differently from an application from an organization they have never heard of, asking a question they consider settled, using credentials they cannot verify.

The filter operates on source, not content. And the result is that certain questions, regardless of their scientific merit, do not make it through the permitting process.

The second feature is the funding structure. Archaeological excavation is expensive. A single season at a major site can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. The money comes, overwhelmingly, from government grants, university research budgets, and institutional endowments. These funding sources have review processes. The review processes evaluate proposals on the basis of their alignment with the field’s research priorities, the credentials of the proposing team, and the likelihood of producing publishable results.

“Publishable results” is the key phrase. A project that confirms the existing chronology of a site will produce results that can be published in the field’s standard journals. A project that challenges the existing chronology faces a publication barrier even before the results are in, because the journals’ editorial boards and peer reviewers are, naturally, composed of researchers who have built careers within the existing framework. A project designed to test whether the tool marks at Sacsayhuaman contain anomalous alloy residues would, even if funded and executed perfectly, face a publication challenge: the result, whether positive or negative, does not fit neatly into any existing research program in mainstream archaeology. A positive result (anomalous alloy found) raises questions the field is not prepared to address. A negative result (no anomalous alloy) contributes to a debate that most archaeologists do not consider worth having.

The funding structure, like the permitting structure, is not designed to suppress dissent. It is designed to allocate scarce resources to research that the field considers productive. The effect, however, is the same: certain questions do not get funded. Certain tests do not get run. Certain observations do not get formalized.

The third feature is the reputational structure. Academic careers are built on reputation. Reputation is built on publication. Publication is built on peer review. Peer review is conducted by established members of the field. An archaeologist who proposes to test the alternative archaeology community’s claims faces a reputational risk that has nothing to do with the quality of the proposed test. The risk is association. By proposing to test the claims, the archaeologist signals that the claims are worth testing, which signals that the claims are worth taking seriously, which signals a departure from the field’s consensus about what is worth taking seriously. The reputational cost is incurred before the test is run, regardless of the result.

This is the mechanism that Sklyarov described as “paradigm protection,” invoking Kuhn’s framework (discussed in Chapter 9) for how scientific paradigms resist disconfirmation not through evidence but through the social structure of the community that holds them. Sklyarov was explicit: he did not believe in a conspiracy. He believed in a system of incentives that produced, through the individual career calculations of thousands of reasonable people, a collective outcome indistinguishable from conspiracy.

The story so far frames the alternative researchers as victims and the institutions as the problem. That framing is too simple, and honesty requires a more complete one.

The alternative researchers have, in many cases, made the institutional response worse through their own behavior. The rhetoric of suppression, of cover-up, of deliberate concealment of evidence, appears throughout the alternative archaeology literature. Erich von Daniken titled one of his books In Search of Ancient Gods: My Pictorial Evidence for the Impossible, which is not the title of a man asking for scientific engagement. Hancock, in the first season of his Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse (2022), characterized mainstream archaeologists as motivated by hostility to his ideas rather than by the ordinary conservatism of a scientific field. He later acknowledged that his rhetoric had been excessive, a concession explored more fully in Chapter 12.

The rhetoric matters because it transforms what could be a productive negotiation into an adversarial confrontation. When an outsider researcher characterizes the institution as corrupt, the institution responds not to the evidence but to the characterization. The evidence disappears into the noise of the confrontation. The test that both sides could benefit from running never gets proposed, because the language in which it would need to be proposed has been poisoned by accusations that no institution can absorb without defensiveness.

Sklyarov was more careful than most. His critique of mainstream archaeology was framed in Kuhnian terms: paradigm protection, institutional incentives, the sociology of knowledge. He did not accuse individual archaeologists of dishonesty. He accused the system of producing outcomes that no individual within it would endorse if asked directly. This is a more precise diagnosis, and it is also a more devastating one, because it locates the problem not in the moral character of the gatekeepers but in the structure they inhabit. You cannot fix a structural problem by replacing the people. You can only fix it by changing the structure.

But even Sklyarov’s careful framing did not produce a different outcome. The tests he specified were never run. The samples he wanted analyzed were never collected. The permitting authorities did not respond to his Kuhnian analysis by opening a dialogue about how to design tests that both mainstream and alternative researchers could endorse. They did not respond at all.

There is one more case worth describing, because it shows The Refusal operating in a context that strips away the objections about credentials, rhetoric, and institutional protocol.

Schoch's credentials were established in Chapter 10: a Yale Ph.D., tenured at Boston University, published in peer-reviewed journals, with his Sphinx analysis presented at the Geological Society of America in 1991. By every institutional metric, he is a credentialed mainstream scientist conducting research within his area of professional expertise.

His claim is specific and testable: the vertical fissures and undulating erosion pattern on the Sphinx body and its enclosure walls are consistent with prolonged rainfall exposure, indicating a construction date thousands of years earlier than the conventional attribution. He has proposed a specific test: core sampling of the enclosure walls to analyze weathering profiles at depth, which would distinguish precipitation-driven weathering from salt-exfoliation or sand-abrasion weathering. The test is non-destructive in any meaningful sense (core samples are small, and the enclosure walls are not decorated surfaces). The test would produce data that both sides of the debate acknowledge would be definitive.

The test has not been performed. Not because Schoch lacks credentials. Not because the test is poorly designed. Not because the site is inaccessible. The Sphinx enclosure is a managed archaeological site with infrastructure already in place for research access. The test has not been performed because the permitting authority, which controls access to the site, has not approved it. And the permitting authority has not explained why.

When the credentialed specialist, working within the normal channels of scientific inquiry, proposing a standard geological test, using recognized methodology, receives the same non-response as the self-funded Russian physicist and the British journalist, the explanation cannot be credentials. The explanation cannot be rhetoric. The explanation cannot be the quality of the proposed test. The explanation is The Refusal.

Marshall's story has an ending, and the ending illuminates something about the timeline of The Refusal.

Marshall published his initial H. pylori findings in 1983 and 1984. He performed his self-experiment in 1984. The NIH formally recognized H. pylori as the primary cause of peptic ulcers in 1994. The Nobel Prize came in 2005. From initial publication to official institutional recognition: eleven years. From initial publication to the highest honor the scientific establishment can bestow: twenty-two years.

Those numbers are worth sitting with. Twenty-two years from evidence to recognition. During those twenty-two years, millions of patients continued to receive treatment for a condition they did not have (acid overproduction) for a cause that was not the real one (stress), while the real cause (a bacterium) and the real cure (antibiotics) were available and had been demonstrated. The Refusal, in the case of H. pylori, had a body count. People did not die in large numbers from untreated ulcers, but they suffered unnecessarily, underwent unnecessary surgeries, took unnecessary medications for decades, and bore the financial burden of chronic treatment when a cure existed.

In archaeology, The Refusal does not have a body count. No one dies because the tool marks at Sacsayhuaman have not been chemically analyzed. No one suffers because the Sphinx enclosure walls have not been core-sampled. The stakes are lower than in medicine, and the urgency is correspondingly lower. This is one of the reasons the gatekeepers can afford to wait. The stones are not going anywhere.

But the researchers are.

Klaus Schmidt, whose encounter with Hancock at Gobekli Tepe was described in Chapter 10, died in 2014. John Anthony West, who translated Schwaller de Lubicz’s observation about the Sphinx erosion into a testable hypothesis and spent decades looking for a geologist to test it, died in 2018. Andrey Sklyarov, who specified the surface-chemistry tests that would confirm or refute his claims about ancient tool technologies, specified those tests in print, in detail, across multiple books. His preliminary findings from Japan were never followed up. His sampling protocols were never executed. His central physical claim remains exactly where it has always been: stated, specified, untested.

The stones are not going anywhere. The people are.

There is a version of this chapter that would end here, on the note of institutional critique, with the reader’s anger directed at the gatekeepers. That version would be satisfying but incomplete, because it would omit the gatekeepers’ best argument, which is also their most uncomfortable one.

The best argument is this: the gatekeeping function is downstream of a legitimate epistemological problem. The alternative archaeology community’s claims are extraordinary. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The evidence that has been presented so far, however extensive, has not been published through peer-reviewed channels, has not been subjected to the standard replication procedures of mainstream science, and has not been collected under conditions that mainstream scientists would recognize as rigorous. Sklyarov’s SEM results are labeled “preliminary” by his own account. His spectral analyses of tool-mark residues cite no laboratory names, no full spectral data, no methodology in replicable form, and no comparison reference database. His photographs, however compelling, are not a substitute for quantitative measurement published in a format that allows independent verification.

The mainstream position, stated most honestly, is not “these claims are wrong.” It is “these claims have not been presented in a form we can evaluate.” And there is something to this. A photograph of a saw cut is not the same as a spectrographic analysis of the kerf residue. A visual observation that a sarcophagus interior shows no light gap against a straightedge is not the same as an interferometric measurement of surface flatness published with error bars and replicated by an independent laboratory. The alternative researchers have gathered observations. They have not, with very few exceptions, produced measurements in the sense that the word “data” is used in quantitative science.

This is a real limitation, and it is fair to name it. But it also brings us back to the circularity. The observations have not been converted to data because the instruments required to make the conversion (portable XRF, scanning electron microscopy, interferometric surface profiling) require either sample access or sustained on-site time, both of which require permits that have not been granted. The observations remain observational because the pathway from observation to measurement runs through an institutional structure that has not opened the gate.

The alternative researchers know this. They have said so, repeatedly, in print and in public. Sklyarov was explicit: the sampling gap was the central limitation of his life’s work, and it was not a limitation of his methodology. It was a limitation of his access. Hancock has made the same point about the underwater sites. Schoch has made it about the Sphinx enclosure.

The mainstream responds: submit the proposal through proper channels. The alternative researchers respond: we have. The proper channels declined.

The loop closes again.

The loop may not have a simple solution. No policy proposal for reforming archaeological permitting structures will appear in these pages. No plan for funding the tests that Sklyarov specified. No mechanism for bridging the gap between the alternative community’s observational record and the mainstream’s quantitative standards.

But the description alone is enough to make a point that should trouble anyone who cares about how knowledge advances.

The point is this: the mainstream critique of alternative archaeology is that its claims are “unscientific.” The word “unscientific” carries a specific meaning. It means: not supported by the methods and standards of empirical science. But the methods and standards of empirical science include testing, and testing requires access, and access is controlled by the institutions that have classified the claims as unscientific. The charge of unsciencefulness is, in many of the most important cases, a charge that can only be answered by evidence that the accusers have prevented from being collected.

This is not an argument that the alternative claims are correct. It is an argument that the word “unscientific” has been used in a way that obscures rather than clarifies. A claim that has been tested and failed is unscientific. A claim that has not been tested because the testing was not permitted is something else. It is untested. And untested is not the same as wrong.

The Refusal is the institutional mechanism that converts “untested” into “unscientific.” It does this not through a single decision but through a thousand small decisions, each one defensible, each one local, each one made by a reasonable person doing a reasonable job within a reasonable system. The aggregate effect of those thousand decisions is that certain questions, certain tests, certain lines of evidence are placed beyond the reach of the scientific method. Not because the method cannot address them. Because the method has not been applied.

Barry Marshall broke through The Refusal by making himself the experiment. He swallowed the bacterium and proved the point with his own body. The alternative archaeology community cannot swallow a megalithic wall. The record is in the stone, and the stone is behind a permitting structure that the community cannot penetrate.

The technology to perform the critical tests non-destructively, without sample collection, without physical contact with the artifact, now exists. Portable XRF can read elemental composition from a stone surface in seconds. Handheld laser-induced breakdown spectrometers can identify alloy residues at parts-per-million sensitivity. Multi-spectral imaging can map surface chemistry across an entire wall face from a distance of several meters. The instruments that Sklyarov would have needed to convert his observations into data now fit in a carry-on bag.

The question is no longer whether the tests can be done. The question is whether anyone will do them. And the answer to that question depends not on the evidence, not on the technology, and not on the scientific merit of the claims. It depends on The Refusal. On whether the thousand small decisions continue to accumulate in the same direction they have accumulated for the last three decades. On whether the filter continues to operate on source rather than content. On whether the architecture of non-investigation remains in place while the questions themselves grow older.

Sklyarov’s tests remain unperformed.

Schoch is still alive. His core-sampling proposal remains unapproved.

Hancock is still alive. His underwater sites remain unexcavated.

The stones remain exactly where they have been for thousands of years, holding whatever record they hold, answering no questions, because no one with the authority to ask has chosen to ask.

The Refusal is not a wall. It is a silence. And in that silence, the evidence sits, patient and indifferent, waiting for someone to break the loop.
