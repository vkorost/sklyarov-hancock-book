# Chapter 8: The Competence Cliff

On the morning of August 4, 2181 BCE, give or take a decade, the last pharaoh of Egypt’s Sixth Dynasty was already dead or irrelevant. His name was probably Nitocris, though the historical record is unreliable on this point because the record was written during the very collapse it describes. What is not in dispute is the date’s neighborhood, because the consequences are visible in the archaeological layer: at some point near the end of the twenty-second century BCE, the Old Kingdom of Egypt stopped functioning.

The word “stopped” is chosen carefully. The Old Kingdom did not decline. It did not gradually lose territory, or slowly hemorrhage administrative capacity, or drift into comfortable mediocrity the way empires often do. It stopped. The monumental construction programs ceased. The central grain storage and distribution systems collapsed. The provincial administrators, who had been managing agriculture and taxation on behalf of the divine pharaoh, began operating independently or not at all. The Nile flood, which had been tracked, predicted, and administratively managed for centuries, continued to rise and fall, but the system that converted the flood into organized agriculture fractured. Egypt entered what Egyptologists call the First Intermediate Period: roughly a century and a half of fragmented authority, regional warlords, famine, and social disorder.

This is not unusual. Civilizations collapse. They have always collapsed. The Romans collapsed. The Maya collapsed. The Minoans collapsed. What is unusual about the Egyptian case is that it did not collapse alone.

At approximately the same time (the dates cluster within a century), the Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia disintegrated. The Harappan civilization of the Indus Valley entered its terminal phase. The BMAC culture of Central Asia (the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, a Bronze Age urban civilization centered in what is now Turkmenistan and Afghanistan) went into decline. And in China, the archaeological record shows a disruption of the early Bronze Age cultures that had been developing in the Yellow River basin.

Five civilizations. Five river systems. Five geographically distinct regions spanning a belt from the Nile to the Yellow River. All entered terminal decline within a window of roughly two centuries, centered on approximately 2200 BCE.

Mainstream archaeology has a name for this: the 4.2 kiloyear event. It refers to a documented global aridification episode, identified in proxy records (lake sediments, cave speleothems, marine cores) across multiple continents. The argument is that a prolonged drought, lasting perhaps three centuries, stressed the agricultural systems of all five civilizations simultaneously. Crops failed. Populations migrated. Central authority, which depended on surplus grain for its legitimacy and its bureaucratic apparatus, lost its economic base. The collapses were independent responses to a shared climatic stressor.

This explanation is not wrong. The aridification is real and documented. But both Hancock and Sklyarov found it insufficient, and their reasons for finding it insufficient are, characteristically, different.

Hancock’s treatment of the 2200 BCE collapse is sparse compared to his treatment of the Younger Dryas catastrophe, which is the organizing event of his framework. But he treats the synchronized timing as significant. If the lost civilization’s survivors had seeded Egypt, Mesopotamia, the Indus Valley, and China with their knowledge, then the civilizations built on that knowledge would share vulnerabilities that independently developed civilizations would not. The synchrony, in Hancock’s reading, is evidence of a common origin: a shared architectural blueprint for civilization, transmitted by the same group of survivors, vulnerable to the same systemic stresses.

Sklyarov went further. Much further.

In his 2016 book Creation of Ancient Civilizations, the final synthesis of his career, Sklyarov devoted sustained attention to the 2200 BCE collapse for a reason that is characteristic of his method: the collapse was, to him, the single strongest piece of evidence that the five civilizations had not developed independently but had been created and managed by a common external authority.

His argument ran as follows. The 4.2 kiloyear climate event, as an explanation for synchronized collapse, requires that five civilizations on five different river systems, with five different agricultural regimes, in five different climate zones, all reached their breaking point simultaneously in response to a drought that affected each system differently. The Nile is a desert river fed by equatorial African rainfall. The Tigris and Euphrates are fed by Anatolian snowmelt. The Indus is fed by Himalayan glaciers and monsoon rainfall. The Yellow River is fed by continental East Asian precipitation. The Central Asian rivers (Amu Darya, Murghab) are fed by the Pamir and Hindu Kush ranges.

A drought severe enough to cause the Nile to fail would not necessarily cause the Indus to fail, because the Indus is fed by an entirely different atmospheric system. A drought that collapsed the Yellow River civilizations would not necessarily affect the Amu Darya basin, which draws from a different mountain range. The five systems are climatically independent. For them all to fail simultaneously, the drought would need to be not merely global but globally uniform in its severity across all five distinct watershed systems. This is possible. It is not, in Sklyarov’s judgment, probable.

His alternative explanation was structural. The five civilizations collapsed simultaneously because they shared a common management infrastructure, and that infrastructure was withdrawn. The gods departed. Whether they left Earth entirely (Sklyarov’s preferred scenario), died out from physiological degradation on a planet whose atmosphere was slowly poisoning them, or destroyed each other in the internal conflict he called the War of Gods, the result was the same: the managing authority that had created these civilizations, imposed their agricultural systems, established their administrative hierarchies, and maintained their technical operations was no longer present. And without the managing authority, the civilizations could not sustain themselves.

I want to give this argument a name, because it describes a phenomenon that extends far beyond ancient history, and the name will carry the chapter.

The Competence Cliff is the moment when a civilization’s technical capability drops not gradually but precipitously, leaving the successor population unable to maintain, repair, or understand the infrastructure it has inherited. The drop is not proportional to the change in resources. It is disproportionate: a small removal of organizing capacity produces a catastrophic loss of operational capability. The cliff is steep because competence in complex systems is not distributed evenly. It is concentrated in a small number of operators, and when those operators are removed, the system does not degrade gracefully. It collapses.

The Competence Cliff is not unique to Sklyarov’s framework. It is a recognizable feature of civilizational transitions throughout recorded history. But Sklyarov’s version of it is more extreme than any historical parallel, because in his model, the competence gap between the departing operators and the remaining population was not a gap between experts and amateurs. It was a gap between species.

To understand the Competence Cliff, consider a case where the historical record is unambiguous and the mechanism is well documented.

In 476 CE, the last Western Roman Emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed by the Germanic chieftain Odoacer. The event is traditionally dated as the “fall” of the Western Roman Empire, though historians rightly point out that the fall was a process, not an event, and that Roman administrative structures persisted in various forms for decades afterward. But for the purposes of this discussion, the relevant question is not when Rome fell. It is what happened to Roman technology after the fall.

The Romans built aqueducts. Some of them still stand. The Pont du Gard, constructed in the first century CE, carried water across the Gardon River valley in southern France with a gradient of thirty-four centimeters per kilometer: a slope of 0.034 percent, maintained over fifty kilometers of channel. The engineering required surveying instruments, hydraulic calculations, and an organizational apparatus capable of coordinating the quarrying, transport, and placement of twenty thousand tons of stone.

When the Roman administrative system that maintained the aqueducts ceased to function, the aqueducts ceased to deliver water. Not because they were destroyed (many survived structurally for centuries) but because they could not be repaired. A leaking channel, a cracked arch, a blocked pipe: these are problems that require specific technical knowledge to diagnose and specific organizational capacity to fix. The post-Roman communities that inherited the aqueducts used them as bridges, as quarries for building stone, as landmarks. They did not use them as aqueducts, because using an aqueduct requires understanding how an aqueduct works, and that understanding departed with the engineers who built it.

The same pattern repeated across Roman technology. The Roman concrete whose loss was noted in Chapter 2 provides a striking case: only in 2023 did a research team at MIT identify the specific mechanism that made it self-healing, the incorporation of lime clasts that allowed cracks to seal themselves on contact with water. The recipe had been lost for over a millennium, and the reason it worked remained unknown for nearly two millennia. Glass: Roman glassmaking techniques, including the production of colorless glass and cameo glass, were lost or severely degraded in post-Roman Europe and did not recover their former quality for roughly eight hundred years.

This is a Competence Cliff. The technology existed. The infrastructure survived. The population that inherited the infrastructure could use it, after a fashion, but could not maintain it, repair it, or replicate it. The gap between having a technology and understanding a technology became visible only when the people who understood it were gone.

Now scale the analogy up.

Both Hancock and Sklyarov documented this same pattern at archaeological sites worldwide, the phenomenon described in Chapter 2 as the Competence Inversion: at every major megalithic site where both ancient and later construction are present, the oldest layers are the most technically sophisticated, and subsequent construction represents a degradation in capability. Sklyarov called it the stratification principle and documented it at every site he visited, across six continents, without exception.

The Competence Cliff is this pattern read as a process rather than a snapshot. The original builders possessed a capability that later populations could not replicate. Those later populations built on top of the original work, reused its blocks, carved their names into its surfaces, and sometimes dismantled it for raw material. But they could not do what the original builders did. They could not cut granite with sub-millimeter precision. They could not fit polygonal blocks without mortar across seismic fault lines. They could not move eight-hundred-ton stones and place them within architectural tolerances that modern construction standards do not require.

The question is: why not?

If the original builders were human (Hancock’s framework), then the Competence Cliff requires an explanation for how human knowledge, once achieved, was lost so completely. Hancock’s explanation is catastrophe: the Younger Dryas impact destroyed the civilization that possessed the knowledge, killed most of its practitioners, and scattered the survivors across a world that could not support the infrastructure needed to maintain the technology. Knowledge transmitted orally and through demonstration degraded over generations, the way a photocopy of a photocopy degrades. What remained was the memory of greatness, encoded in mythology and preserved in the most durable medium available: stone.

If the original builders were non-human (Sklyarov’s framework), then the Competence Cliff does not require a separate explanation at all. The knowledge was never human to begin with. It belonged to the gods. When the gods departed, the knowledge departed with them. The remaining human population could no more replicate the gods’ construction than a Melanesian villager could build a functioning radio from bamboo and coconut shells. The competence was not lost. It was withdrawn. And the population left behind entered precisely the condition that the cargo cult metaphor describes: a community preserving the forms of a superior technology without the function, building cruder imitations of structures whose engineering they admired but could not understand.

I said in the previous chapter that Sklyarov’s model of the ancient world had a structural resonance with the world that produced him. I want to return to this, briefly and lightly, because the Competence Cliff is the specific point where the resonance is most visible.

In December 1991, the Soviet Union dissolved. The event was, like the end of the Old Kingdom, less a single moment than a process, but the process was remarkably compressed: within two years, the centralized authority that had managed the economic, military, and administrative apparatus of one-sixth of the earth’s landmass ceased to function.

What followed was, in miniature, a Competence Cliff. The centralized system had managed industrial production, agricultural distribution, scientific research, military logistics, and civilian infrastructure through a hierarchy of planners, administrators, and technical specialists who reported, ultimately, to Moscow. When the central authority dissolved, the hierarchy lost its organizing apex. Factory managers who had received production orders from the ministry now received no orders. Engineers who had requisitioned materials through central supply channels found the channels empty. Agricultural collectives that had shipped grain to central distribution nodes found the nodes abandoned.

The infrastructure survived, mostly. The factories still stood. The railways still ran, for a time. The nuclear arsenal was still there, though questions about who controlled it kept Western defense planners awake for several years. But the competence to operate the infrastructure as a system, to coordinate the thousands of interdependent decisions that keep a continental economy functioning, was concentrated in the central apparatus, and the central apparatus was gone.

This is not a thesis about Sklyarov’s motivations. I am not arguing that he modeled the ancient gods’ departure on the Soviet collapse. The chronology does not support that reading in any case: Sklyarov’s paleocontact hypothesis predated the Soviet dissolution, and his 2016 treatment of the synchronized collapse drew on archaeological data, not personal experience. But I am observing that a man who grew up inside a system where a remote central authority managed a vast population through bureaucratic structures, and who then witnessed the consequences when that authority dissolved, produced a model of ancient civilization in which a remote central authority managed a vast population through bureaucratic structures, and the consequences of that authority’s dissolution were the defining event of ancient history.

The parallel is not proof of anything. It is a pattern. And patterns, in a book about two researchers who saw different patterns in the same stones, are what we are here to notice.

There is a specific feature of the Competence Cliff that both Hancock and Sklyarov documented but interpreted differently, and it is worth examining because it sharpens the divergence between their frameworks.

Consider the Sumerian temple economy.

The earliest Sumerian cities (Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Lagash) were organized around temple complexes. The temple was not merely a place of worship. It was the economic center of the city. All land was, in the earliest phase, temple land. All craftsmen were temple employees. All traders were temple agents. The granaries belonged to the temple. The irrigation systems were managed by the temple. The scribes who kept the records, the administrators who organized labor, the priests who performed the rituals: all were part of the temple apparatus.

This is well documented in mainstream Sumerology. What is less commonly emphasized is the implication that Sklyarov drew from it: the Sumerian temple economy was not a spontaneous development. It did not emerge from the bottom up, through the gradual accumulation of private property and the subsequent development of administrative structures to manage it. It was imposed from the top down. The temple came first. Private property developed later, emerging only after the temple system began to weaken.

Sklyarov pointed out that this sequence contradicts the standard materialist model of state formation. Friedrich Engels, in The Origin of the Family, Private Property, and the State (1884), argued that the state arose as an instrument of class rule, created by property-owning elites to protect their accumulated wealth. The sequence was: surplus production leads to private property, private property leads to class stratification, class stratification leads to the state. Sklyarov’s reading of the Sumerian evidence inverted this: the state (the temple) existed before private property, organized production before surplus was accumulated, and managed the population before class distinctions emerged.

For Sklyarov, this inversion was evidence that the organizing authority was external. The temple economy was not built by the people it managed. It was built for them, by the gods, as an administrative system for organizing human labor in service of the gods’ requirements. The priests were not spiritual leaders who had risen to authority through charisma or tradition. They were administrators, appointed by the gods, who managed the production and distribution of agricultural products (especially grain and fermented beverages) that the gods required for their biochemistry.

Hancock would read the same temple economy differently. The survivors of the lost civilization, arriving among less technologically developed populations, established organizational structures that reflected their own civilizational knowledge. The temple complexes were not imposed by non-human operators but designed by human teachers who understood that organized agriculture, centralized storage, and systematic record-keeping were necessary for civilizational survival. The god-as-administrator was a cultural memory of the human teacher-as-administrator, elevated to divine status over centuries of transmission.

Same temple. Same grain. Same scribal records on the same clay tablets. Two readings. And the Competence Cliff appears in both: when the organizing authority departed (whether human survivors who died out or non-human operators who left), the system they built could not sustain itself. The temple economy collapsed not because the grain stopped growing or the rivers stopped flowing but because the people who knew how to run the system were gone, and the people who remained had been operating within the system, not understanding it.

There is a lateral example from modern organizational theory that captures the Competence Cliff with uncomfortable precision.

In the 1990s, the management theorist Ikujiro Nonaka, at Hitotsubashi University in Tokyo, developed a framework for understanding how knowledge functions in organizations. Nonaka distinguished between two types of knowledge: explicit knowledge (knowledge that can be codified, written down, and transmitted in documents) and tacit knowledge (knowledge that cannot be fully articulated, that resides in the practitioner’s experience, judgment, and skill, and that can be transmitted only through apprenticeship, observation, and practice).

A recipe is explicit knowledge. The ability to cook well is tacit knowledge. An operations manual is explicit knowledge. The judgment to know when to deviate from the manual is tacit knowledge. The distinction matters because organizations that lose their experienced practitioners lose their tacit knowledge, and tacit knowledge is, by definition, the knowledge that cannot be recovered from documents.

Nonaka’s framework was developed for corporate settings: what happens when a company’s most experienced engineers retire, or when a factory’s production team is replaced. But it applies to the Competence Cliff with eerie precision. The ancient builders, whether human or non-human, possessed enormous tacit knowledge: not just the specifications for cutting granite (explicit knowledge that could, in principle, be written down) but the embodied skill of operating the tools, the judgment to adapt technique to material, the experience of a thousand previous cuts that tells the operator when the blade needs replacement before it breaks.

When the builders departed, the explicit knowledge survived, in degraded form, in mythological texts and ritual procedures. The Maintenance Manual from the previous chapter is a catalog of explicit knowledge preserved after the tacit knowledge was lost. But the tacit knowledge departed with the operators. And without the tacit knowledge, the explicit knowledge was not sufficient to reproduce the technology. You can have the recipe and still not be able to cook.

This is why the Competence Cliff is steep. It is not a gradual loss of capability. It is the sudden absence of the practitioners who carried the irreducible, untransferable component of the technology. The apprentices are gone because the masters left. The line of transmission is broken. And what remains is not a degraded version of the original skill but something qualitatively different: a ritual, a memory, a cargo-cult imitation that preserves the form without the function.

I want to be careful about the emotional register of this chapter, because the Competence Cliff, in Sklyarov’s framework, implies something about human history that most people would rather not contemplate.

In Hancock’s framework, the Competence Cliff is a tragedy. A great human civilization was destroyed by catastrophe, and its survivors did their best to transmit what they knew to populations who could not fully receive it. The loss was terrible but not total. The knowledge degraded, but fragments survived in mythology, in astronomical alignments, in the persistent memory of a golden age. Humanity is not permanently diminished. The knowledge can, in principle, be recovered. The underwater sites can be explored. The myths can be decoded. The astronomical alignments can be read. The Competence Cliff is a gap in human history, not a permanent feature of the human condition.

In Sklyarov’s framework, the Competence Cliff is not a tragedy. It is a structural feature. Humans were never the operators. They were the workforce. The gap between the gods’ capability and human capability was not a gap that education or time could close, because it was not a gap in knowledge. It was a gap in species. The gods possessed technology that was designed for their physiology, their cognitive architecture, their metallurgical traditions. Humans could not learn to operate that technology any more than a dog could learn to drive a car. The dog can ride in the car. The dog can recognize the car. The dog may even develop strong feelings about the car. But the dog cannot drive the car, because the car was not designed for dogs.

The Competence Cliff, in this reading, is permanent. It is the defining condition of post-divine human civilization. Everything humans built after the gods departed was built within human capability, which is to say, at a level orders of magnitude below what the gods achieved. The pyramids humans built (the late, shoddy ones, with rubble cores and collapsing corridors) were the best they could do. The rituals they performed were the most they could recover of the operating procedures. The religions they founded were the most coherent narratives they could construct to explain why the world had stopped working the way it used to work.

This is, to repeat what I said in Chapter 5, a bleak reading. But it is not a nihilistic one. There is, in Sklyarov’s late work, a thread of something that might be called respect for human ingenuity within human limitations. He documented, with evident admiration, the precision of the Inca stonework that sits atop the gods’ foundations at Sacsayhuaman. It is not as good as what lies beneath it. It is not even close. But it is the best that human hands could achieve, and it is very good. The Inca masons who fitted those upper courses were not failed gods. They were accomplished humans, working at the boundary of human capability, and their work deserves to be evaluated on its own terms, not against a standard set by a different species.

Let me return to the synchronized collapse of 2200 BCE, because there is a detail in the archaeological record that both researchers found significant and that illuminates the Competence Cliff from a different angle.

After the Old Kingdom collapsed in Egypt, and after the Akkadian Empire collapsed in Mesopotamia, and after the Harappan civilization entered its decline, something interesting happened in each case. The populations did not disappear. They did not die out. They continued to farm, to build, to organize themselves into communities. But the scale of their organizational achievement dropped sharply, and it dropped in a specific way.

In Egypt, the First Intermediate Period produced no monumental architecture, no organized pyramid-building programs, no large-scale irrigation projects managed by central authority. The local nomarchs (provincial governors) maintained order within their provinces, but the inter-provincial coordination that had defined the Old Kingdom was gone. When Egypt eventually reunified under the Middle Kingdom (approximately 2055 BCE), the new kingdom’s achievements, while substantial, never matched the Old Kingdom’s most extraordinary constructions. The Great Pyramid was not replicated. The Serapeum sarcophagi were not replicated. The finest work of the Old Kingdom remained, in perpetuity, the finest work.

In Mesopotamia, the post-Akkadian period produced the Third Dynasty of Ur, which rebuilt much of the administrative apparatus. But the Ur III period lasted barely a century before collapsing in turn, and the subsequent Old Babylonian period, while culturally rich, did not produce construction or administrative achievements on the scale of the Akkadian peak.

In the Indus Valley, the post-Harappan “Cemetery H” culture occupied the same sites but at a reduced level of urban sophistication. The standardized weights, measures, and urban planning that had characterized the mature Harappan phase (standardized brick sizes across a territory of over a million square kilometers, a feat of administrative coordination that modern building codes would struggle to replicate) were not maintained.

The pattern is consistent. After the collapse, the population recovered, rebuilt, and achieved a new equilibrium. But the new equilibrium was at a lower level of organizational and technical capability than the pre-collapse peak. The Competence Cliff was not a temporary disruption. It was a permanent reset. The populations climbed back to a plateau, but the plateau was lower than where they had been.

Sklyarov’s explanation: the pre-collapse peak reflected the gods’ managerial and technical capability. The post-collapse plateau reflected human capability operating independently for the first time. The difference between the two levels is the measure of the gods’ contribution. And the contribution, once removed, could not be replaced, because it was not knowledge that had been shared with humans. It was capability that had been exercised on behalf of humans by beings who did not transfer it because they did not intend for humans to operate independently.

Hancock’s explanation: the pre-collapse peak reflected the accumulated knowledge of the lost civilization’s survivors, transmitted over millennia but degrading with each generation of transmission. The post-collapse plateau reflected the point at which the transmitted knowledge had degraded below the threshold of usefulness. The survivors’ descendants had forgotten enough that they could no longer maintain the systems their ancestors had built. The Competence Cliff was the point where the last generation with living memory of the original knowledge died or lost its influence.

Both explanations account for the same pattern. Both predict the stratification principle. Both explain why the oldest layers at megalithic sites are the best. The difference between them is the identity of the departing competence. Was it human knowledge, degrading through imperfect transmission? Or was it non-human capability, removed by the departure of its possessors?

There is one more dimension of the Competence Cliff that I want to examine before this chapter closes, because it connects to something I have noticed about both researchers that neither of them, as far as I can tell, noticed about himself.

Both Hancock and Sklyarov spent their careers documenting a Competence Cliff in the ancient world. And both, in different ways, operated on the modern side of a Competence Cliff of their own.

Hancock could not read Sklyarov’s thirty-two books, because they were written in Russian. The entire Russian-language alternative history corpus, the largest body of independent field-based investigation of megalithic anomalies ever produced by a single researcher, was invisible to him. He operated within the Anglophone world, with access to Anglophone sources, and the richest parallel investigation of his own subject was on the other side of a linguistic barrier he could not cross.

Sklyarov could not access the institutional resources that would have tested his most important claims. The X-ray mass spectrometry of tool-mark residues that he documented in his later books was conducted by a small team of collaborators with limited equipment. The comprehensive, multi-site, peer-reviewed chemical analysis that would have settled the question of tool composition (human metallurgical tradition or not?) was beyond his reach, not because the technology did not exist but because the institutions that controlled the technology would not perform the tests he requested. He operated on the wrong side of an institutional barrier.

Both barriers are Competence Cliffs of a modern kind. Not a loss of knowledge but a loss of access. The competence exists. The instruments exist. The technology to perform the tests, to translate the books, to synthesize the evidence across languages and institutional boundaries, exists. But the organizational infrastructure that would bring the competence to bear on the questions both researchers identified does not exist, or did not exist during their lifetimes.

This is the most important observation to carry out of this chapter. The Competence Cliff is not only an ancient phenomenon. It is a description of any system in which critical capability is concentrated in a small number of actors, and the removal or inaccessibility of those actors produces a disproportionate loss of function. It happens when gods withdraw from ancient civilizations. It happens when empires collapse and their engineering traditions are lost. It happens when a Russian physicist’s thirty-two books are invisible to an English-speaking world because no one has translated them. And it happens when the institutions that could settle the most important archaeological questions of our time decline to perform the tests.

The cliff is not about the height of the fall. It is about the concentration of competence. When competence is concentrated, the system is fragile. When the concentration point is removed, the system does not degrade proportionally. It collapses. And the population left on the other side of the collapse is left with infrastructure it cannot maintain, texts it cannot read, and questions it cannot answer.

There is a cemetery in the Egyptian desert that deserves attention here.

It is at Abydos, in Upper Egypt, in the shadow of the Osirion: the massive, austere granite structure whose construction has never been satisfactorily dated or explained within conventional Egyptology. The Osirion sits below ground level, its enormous blocks of red granite fitted without mortar, its columns weighing a hundred tons each, its architectural style utterly unlike anything else from the Nineteenth Dynasty to which it is nominally attributed.

Surrounding the Osirion, above and around and adjacent to it, is the ordinary Egyptian cemetery. The tombs are made of mud brick. They are small, rectangular, functional. They held the bodies of people who lived and died in the centuries after the Osirion was built, people who walked past its granite columns every day, who may have prayed there, who certainly knew it was old and important and unlike anything they could build themselves.

The contrast between the Osirion and the surrounding cemetery is the Competence Cliff made visible. Below: granite blocks of a hundred tons, fitted to tolerances that modern construction does not require. Above: mud brick, carefully made, structurally sound, entirely within human capability. Below: a technology whose mechanism is unknown. Above: a technology whose mechanism is a mold and a trowel. The two construction traditions are separated by perhaps a millennium and by a chasm in capability that no known technological development bridges.

The people buried in those mud-brick tombs were not inferior to the builders of the Osirion. They were different. They operated within a different capability envelope, using different tools, working with different materials, solving different problems. Their construction was adequate for its purpose. Their lives were complete. They were not living in the ruins of someone else’s achievement. They were living in the neighborhood of it, which is a different thing.

This is the human condition that the Competence Cliff describes, regardless of whether you accept Hancock’s framework or Sklyarov’s. We live in the neighborhood of achievements we did not make and cannot replicate. We walk past structures whose engineering exceeds anything in our experience. We tell stories about the builders that reflect who we are more than who the builders were. And we build our own structures, in our own materials, with our own skills, knowing that they will not match what came before but hoping that they will be adequate for what comes after.

The people in the mud-brick tombs at Abydos did not spend their lives in despair because they could not build the Osirion. They spent their lives farming, raising children, worshipping their gods, and dying in the ordinary way. The Competence Cliff was behind them. They faced forward.

Both Hancock and Sklyarov, each in his own way, spent a career facing backward: toward the cliff, toward the question of what was up there before the fall. Both asked who built the structures at the top. Both asked how the capability was lost. Both asked whether the loss was permanent.

The tests that would answer those questions remain unperformed. The cliff remains. The questions remain. And the mud-brick world on this side of it, the world we inhabit, continues to build what it can with the tools it has, in the neighborhood of something extraordinary that it did not make and cannot yet explain.
