# Chapter 10: The Invisible College

Robert Schoch is a careful man. You can see this in the way he writes, which is the way a geologist writes: measurements first, interpretations second, qualifications throughout. He is an associate professor of natural sciences at Boston University, where he has tenure. He holds a Ph.D. in geology and geophysics from Yale. He is not the kind of person who wakes up one morning and decides to overturn the timeline of Egyptian civilization.

What happened was that someone asked him to look at a rock.

The someone was John Anthony West, a self-taught Egyptologist from New York who had spent years studying the work of R.A. Schwaller de Lubicz, a French philosopher who had spent fifteen years at Luxor and had noticed, in passing, that the erosion pattern on the body of the Great Sphinx looked more like water damage than wind damage. West read Schwaller’s observation, recognized that he lacked the geological credentials to evaluate it himself, and went looking for a geologist who would.

He found Schoch. Schoch looked at the rock. The rock was the Sphinx.

In 1991, Schoch presented his findings at the Geological Society of America annual meeting. His conclusion: the vertical fissures and undulating surface erosion on the Sphinx body and its surrounding enclosure trench were consistent with prolonged exposure to heavy rainfall, not wind-blown sand. Since the Giza Plateau has not experienced significant rainfall since the end of the last Ice Age, the original carving of the Sphinx must predate the conventional attribution to the Fourth Dynasty (approximately 2500 BCE) by thousands of years. His initial estimate placed the construction at approximately 7,000 to 9,000 BCE. By 2024, he had refined this to approximately 12,000 to 12,500 years ago, coinciding with the end of the Younger Dryas.

The finding traveled. It traveled because Schoch was credentialed, because the method was geological (not mythological, not archaeological, not astrological), and because the implication was enormous: if the Sphinx was that old, then someone was carving monumental stone thousands of years before the earliest known civilization. It traveled to Graham Hancock, who built an entire chapter of Fingerprints of the Gods around it. It traveled to Andrey Sklyarov, who cited Schoch in six of his thirty-two books. It traveled to Robert Bauval, who used it to anchor the Orion Correlation Theory. It traveled to the alternative archaeology community worldwide, where it became one of the most frequently cited single findings in the field.

But here is the thing I want you to notice. The finding did not originate with Schoch. It originated with Schwaller de Lubicz, who noticed the erosion pattern in the 1940s. It was rescued from Schwaller’s dense, esoteric prose by West, who translated the observation into a testable hypothesis. It was tested by Schoch, who had the credentials and the methodology. It was then adopted by Hancock, who had the audience. It was independently adopted by Sklyarov, who had a different audience in a different language on a different continent.

This is a chain. It has five links. Each link added something that the previous link lacked: Schwaller had the observation but not the hypothesis. West had the hypothesis but not the credentials. Schoch had the credentials but not the audience. Hancock had the English-language audience. Sklyarov had the Russian-language audience. The chain spans three countries, two languages, five decades, and zero institutions. No university department coordinated the research. No funding agency paid for it. No journal published the sequence as a coherent program of inquiry. The chain assembled itself, link by link, through the decisions of individual researchers who recognized the value of what the previous link had produced.

I want to give this phenomenon a name, because the name describes something larger than any individual chain.

In the early 1640s, a group of natural philosophers in England began meeting informally to discuss experimental science. They had no institutional home. They were not affiliated with Oxford or Cambridge (though some held positions there). They had no charter, no bylaws, no budget, and no formal membership criteria. They met in each other’s rooms, in coffeehouses, and sometimes in the rooms above an apothecary’s shop on Wood Street in London. They called themselves, with a self-awareness that transcended their era, the Invisible College.

The members included Robert Boyle, who would formulate the gas law that bears his name. Robert Hooke, who would discover cells and formulate the law of elasticity. Christopher Wren, who would design St. Paul’s Cathedral and contribute to the mathematical foundations of architecture. John Wilkins, who would later become the first secretary of the institution the group eventually founded. They were, by any measure, among the most productive scientists of the seventeenth century. And for the first two decades of their collaboration, they had no official existence.

In 1660, the Invisible College became visible. It received a royal charter from Charles II and became the Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge, which remains one of the most prestigious scientific institutions in the world. The transition from informal network to formal institution is, in the standard telling, a success story: a group of outsiders proved their value and were rewarded with legitimacy.

But there is a detail in this story that the standard telling omits. The Invisible College did not need the charter to produce its best work. Boyle’s experiments on the spring of the air were conducted before the Royal Society existed. Hooke’s microscopical observations were underway before anyone gave him a title. The work came first. The institution came second. The charter legitimized what had already been accomplished. It did not create the accomplishment.

I want to borrow the name, because the alternative archaeology community is an Invisible College. It is a loose, unfunded, cross-disciplinary network of researchers who share evidence, visit each other’s sites, cite each other’s work, and build on each other’s findings without institutional support, peer review, or academic incentive. They are conducting a distributed research program with no coordination, no budget, and no formal structure. The question the name raises is the obvious one: is this an Invisible College that will eventually be legitimized, the way Boyle’s circle became the Royal Society? Or is it an Invisible College that will remain invisible, not because it lacks the quality to become visible but because the modern institutional structure has no mechanism for granting legitimacy to research conducted outside its walls?

The answer is not yet clear. But the network can be mapped, because the citations are traceable.

Let me start with the map, because the map is the evidence.

A systematic reading of the complete corpora of both Hancock and Sklyarov yields indexes of every person cited, every work referenced, and every stance taken toward each source. The indexes are long (Sklyarov’s alone runs to hundreds of entries across thirty-two books). But the structure they reveal is legible and consistent, and it tells a story about how knowledge moves through an unofficial network.

Here is the central finding. Despite working in different languages on different continents with no documented direct communication, Hancock and Sklyarov cite many of the same people. Not the same ancient sources (Plato, Manetho, the Sumerian king lists; everyone cites those). The same modern researchers. The same living (or recently living) investigators. The same names appear in the footnotes of books written in English in Bath and books written in Russian in Moscow, and the pattern of citation is too specific to be coincidental.

Robert Schoch appears in both corpora. Hancock cites him in six of his eight major books and four interview transcripts. Sklyarov cites him in six of his thirty-two works. Both treat him as a credentialed mainstream ally whose geological analysis of the Sphinx provides independent validation for an older-than-conventional timeline. Neither Hancock nor Sklyarov developed the Sphinx water-erosion hypothesis. Both adopted it from Schoch, who adopted it from West, who adopted it from Schwaller de Lubicz. The chain is five links long, and its final two links were added independently.

Christopher Dunn appears in both corpora, though in different roles. Hancock cites Dunn occasionally, for the Serapeum precision measurements. Sklyarov cites Dunn in six books and treats him as “the most frequently cited supporting engineering authority for the machine-tool hypothesis.” Dunn is an aerospace machinist by training, not an academic. His contribution was to apply the quality-control methodology of modern precision manufacturing to the stone surfaces at Giza and to conclude that the tolerances exceeded what hand tools could produce. Sklyarov adopted Dunn’s analysis wholesale. Hancock adopted it selectively. Both treated it as evidence, not authority: Dunn’s credentials as a machinist, not as an archaeologist, were the point.

John Anthony West appears in both corpora. Hancock cites him in five sources. Sklyarov cites him in four books. Both credit him as the bridge between Schwaller de Lubicz and Schoch. West had no academic credentials. His authority derived entirely from the quality of his question and from his success in finding a credentialed geologist to answer it.

Flinders Petrie, the founder of modern Egyptological methodology, appears in both corpora. Hancock cites him as a standard scholarly reference. Sklyarov cites him in seven books and treats him as “the primary archaeological authority for the machine-tool hypothesis,” describing Petrie as having “reached correct conclusions a hundred years ago that Egyptologists then buried.” The claim is specific: Petrie measured spiral drill grooves with a pitch of one to two millimeters in granite cores from Giza and concluded that the drilling required loads of one to two tons, implying machine-level force. This measurement, made in 1883, is the oldest piece of evidence in either researcher’s arsenal, and both researchers treat it as foundational.

Zecharia Sitchin appears in both corpora, with different postures. Hancock keeps Sitchin at a stable distance, citing him rarely and without endorsement. Sklyarov cites Sitchin in seven books, using his Sumerian translations as data while rejecting his “Planet Nibiru” framework. The relationship evolves: by 2016, Sklyarov had moved closer to Sitchin’s overall scenario while maintaining specific disagreements. The evolution is documented across the corpus, which is the point: the citation network is not static. It changes over time, and the changes reveal intellectual movement.

Robert Bauval appears frequently in Hancock’s corpus (they co-authored three books) but also appears in Sklyarov’s, where the posture is more complex. Sklyarov used Bauval’s Sphinx-dating material positively but used his own astronomical software (StarCal) to explicitly refute the Orion Correlation Theory. This is a characteristic Invisible College behavior: use the data, reject the interpretation, and say so openly. There is no social pressure to maintain collegial harmony, because there is no college.

Erich von Daniken appears in both corpora, treated by both with a mixture of acknowledgment and distance. Hancock rarely cites him. Sklyarov cites him in six books and explicitly notes that Daniken’s framework was “pioneering but not scholarly.” Both researchers seem aware that Daniken is, for the mainstream, the paradigm case of what alternative archaeology looks like, and both take care to distinguish their work from his. The distancing is itself a form of network behavior: within the Invisible College, reputation management operates through citation posture, not institutional hierarchy.

Charles Hapgood appears in both corpora, and the citation histories diverge dramatically. Hancock adopted Hapgood’s crustal displacement theory as foundational in 1995, then publicly abandoned it in 1999, and by 2015 had replaced it entirely with the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis. Sklyarov adopted Hapgood’s crustal displacement model as the physical mechanism for the Flood event and never abandoned it. Both used Hapgood’s ancient maps analysis. The divergence on crustal displacement is one of the specific points where the two corpora, despite sharing so many common sources, reach different conclusions from the same input. Hancock decided the mechanism was wrong and replaced it. Sklyarov decided it was right and retained it.

The pattern becomes clearer when you step back from individual citations and look at the network as a whole.

The Invisible College of alternative archaeology has a structure. It is not the structure of an academic department, with a chairman, junior faculty, and graduate students arranged in a hierarchy. It is the structure of a web: distributed, non-hierarchical, with multiple nodes of varying importance connected by citation links of varying strength. Some nodes are central (Schoch, Dunn, Petrie, Sitchin). Some nodes are peripheral (researchers cited once in a single book for a single data point). The central nodes are defined not by their institutional authority (most have little) but by their citation frequency: the number of times other researchers in the network reference their work.

And the network has a property that academic citation networks share but that alternative archaeology’s network expresses more visibly: it is organized not by discipline but by site.

In academic archaeology, the citation network organizes around departments, journals, and theoretical frameworks. You cite people in your field. You cite people who publish in your journals. You cite people whose theoretical commitments align with yours. The organizing principle is institutional.

In the Invisible College, the organizing principle is geographic. The network clusters around sites: Giza, the Sphinx, Sacsayhuaman, Baalbek, Gobekli Tepe, Ollantaytambo, Teotihuacan, Puma Punku. Researchers who have visited the same site cite each other regardless of theoretical commitment, language, or methodology. Hancock (a journalist) cites Schoch (a geologist) because both have been to the Sphinx enclosure. Sklyarov (a physicist) cites Dunn (an aerospace machinist) because both have measured the sarcophagi at the Serapeum. The physical site is the meeting point, and the citation is the record of the meeting.

This is not how academic networks normally work. In academic networks, you cite people you agree with (or people you are arguing against, which is a different kind of agreement: the agreement that the question matters). In the Invisible College, you cite people who have been where you have been. The evidence is the site. The site generates the citation. The citation builds the network.

There is a second structural feature of the Invisible College that the citation indexes reveal, and it is more surprising than the first.

The network is asymmetric.

Hancock is cited by Sklyarov. Sklyarov is not cited by Hancock. This asymmetry is not subtle. Hancock’s name appears in ten of Sklyarov’s thirty-two works. He is referenced for megafaunal extinction evidence, Egyptian agricultural data, Sphinx erosion, the Orion correlation, Yonaguni, and various comparative analyses. Sklyarov treats Hancock as what he is: a useful compiler of evidence whose specific interpretations must be independently verified. The posture is one of selective agreement, which is the standard posture of a researcher using another researcher’s data.

Hancock, by contrast, never cites Sklyarov. Not once, in eight major books and four documented interviews spanning 1992 to 2024. Sklyarov’s name does not appear in Hancock’s indexes, footnotes, acknowledgments, or oral presentations. The thirty-two books written in Russian do not exist in Hancock’s documented intellectual world.

This is not, as far as I can determine, a deliberate exclusion. It is a language barrier. Hancock reads English. Sklyarov wrote in Russian. The two corpora exist in separate linguistic ecosystems, and the flow of information between them is unidirectional: Sklyarov read Hancock (in Russian translation; Fingerprints of the Gods was published in Russian as Sledy Bogov), but Hancock, as far as the record shows, never read Sklyarov.

The asymmetry is illuminating because it demonstrates something about the Invisible College that distinguishes it from its seventeenth-century predecessor. Boyle, Hooke, Wren, and Wilkins all spoke the same language, lived in the same country, and could meet in the same coffeehouse. Their Invisible College was invisible to the public but visible to itself. The alternative archaeology network is invisible in a different way. It is invisible even to its own members. Hancock and Sklyarov were, in a sense, colleagues who never met, collaborators who never collaborated, members of the same research community who did not know they were in the same community. They read some of the same sources, visited some of the same sites, reached some of the same conclusions, and diverged on others, and they did all of this without ever exchanging a word.

The implications are worth noting. If two researchers, working independently in different languages with no communication, converge on the same physical observations (construction quality stratification, tool-mark anomalies, the inadequacy of conventional explanations for the largest megalithic constructions), then either both are making the same mistake for independent reasons, or both are observing something real. The convergence does not prove that the observations are correct. But it does establish that the observations are not the product of social contagion, groupthink, or mutual reinforcement, because there was no social contact to produce contagion, no group to produce groupthink, and no communication channel through which reinforcement could flow.

This is, paradoxically, an advantage of the Invisible College’s dysfunctional structure. Academic researchers working on the same problem routinely influence each other through conferences, journal exchanges, and informal communication. Their convergence, when it occurs, is partly a product of this influence. Two researchers who have never communicated and who converge anyway have provided a natural experiment in independent replication. The replication is imperfect (both may have been influenced by common third-party sources, such as Schoch or Dunn), but it is more independent than any collaboration would be.

I want to map one more feature of the network, because it reveals something that the citation indexes alone do not show.

The Invisible College has a generational structure.

The first generation consists of researchers who worked before the alternative archaeology community existed as a recognizable entity. Schwaller de Lubicz (1887 to 1961) spent fifteen years at Luxor, noticed the water erosion on the Sphinx, and published the observation in a book about Egyptian sacred science that almost nobody read. Flinders Petrie (1853 to 1942) measured the drill grooves at Giza in 1883 and concluded that machine technologies were required, an observation that his own discipline ignored for a century. Arthur Posnansky (1873 to 1946) spent fifty years at Tiahuanaco and proposed astronomical alignment dates that placed the site’s construction at approximately 15,000 BCE. These are the precursors: researchers who gathered observations that had no community to receive them.

The second generation is the bridge generation: researchers who took the precursors’ observations and translated them into testable hypotheses. John Anthony West took Schwaller’s observation about the Sphinx and turned it into a geological question. Christopher Dunn took Petrie’s measurements and reanalyzed them using the precision standards of modern aerospace manufacturing. Charles Hapgood took medieval maps and proposed that they encoded Ice Age geographic knowledge. These bridge figures are not, in most cases, credentialed specialists. West was self-taught. Dunn was a machinist. Hapgood was a history professor. Their contribution was not expertise. It was translation: they converted observations from one disciplinary language into questions that could be answered in another.

The third generation is the synthesis generation: researchers who assembled the translated observations into comprehensive frameworks. This is where Hancock and Sklyarov sit. Both drew on the work of the precursors and the bridge figures. Both integrated observations from multiple sites, multiple disciplines, and multiple researchers into unified accounts. Both produced the bulk of the alternative archaeology community’s published output. And both operated in a peculiar professional position: they were the most visible representatives of a research community that had no official existence.

The fourth generation is emerging now, though it is too early to characterize it fully. It includes researchers who are using technologies that were not available to earlier generations: LiDAR surveys of Amazonian geoglyphs, ancient DNA analysis, advanced imaging of underwater sites, and the digital tools that make it possible for anyone with an internet connection to access satellite imagery, geological databases, and archaeological archives that were previously locked behind institutional paywalls. This generation has a relationship to the Invisible College that the earlier generations did not: they can access the work without having to find it through personal networks or obscure publishing channels. The digital infrastructure that makes Sklyarov’s Russian-language works accessible (the Laboratory of Alternative History website, which hosts the complete corpus) did not exist when Schwaller was measuring erosion patterns at Luxor.

The generational structure matters because it answers a question that the Invisible College’s critics frequently raise: if the alternative archaeology community has been making these claims for decades, why has no one proven them or disproven them? The answer, visible in the generational structure, is that the research program is younger than it appears. Schwaller noticed the erosion in the 1940s. Schoch tested it in 1991. That is a gap of fifty years between observation and first test, a gap caused entirely by the absence of an institutional mechanism that could have connected an esoteric French philosopher with an American geologist in less than half a century. The Invisible College does not have a slow publication cycle. It has an impossibly slow recruitment cycle, because every new member must find the community by accident, and the accidents are rare.

There is a lateral case from academic science that clarifies what I am describing.

In the 1970s, a mathematical physicist named Benoit Mandelbrot began publishing papers on a class of geometric objects he called fractals: shapes that exhibited self-similarity at every scale, so that a small piece of the shape, magnified, looked identical to the whole. Mandelbrot did not invent fractals. The mathematical foundations had been laid decades earlier by Hausdorff, Julia, and Fatou. Mandelbrot’s contribution was synthesis: he recognized that these mathematical objects appeared in nature (coastlines, clouds, blood vessels, river networks) and that the existing mathematical frameworks could be unified under a single conceptual umbrella.

Mandelbrot’s position was, for years, precarious. He was not a pure mathematician (they did not consider his work rigorous enough). He was not an applied scientist (his work was too abstract for application). He was not in any department (he worked at IBM’s research center, which gave him freedom but not academic legitimacy). He was, in effect, a member of a one-person Invisible College: conducting a distributed research program across multiple disciplines, drawing on work that no established discipline claimed as its own, and producing a synthesis that no established discipline knew how to evaluate.

The parallels to the alternative archaeology network are structural. In both cases, observations gathered across multiple disciplines went unconnected because no institutional structure existed to connect them. In both cases, the synthesis was produced by someone operating outside the established hierarchy. In both cases, the initial reception was dismissal, followed by gradual acceptance as the evidence accumulated beyond the point of comfortable denial.

But there is one critical difference. Mandelbrot’s fractals were mathematically verifiable. A fractal dimension can be calculated, and the calculation can be checked by anyone with the training to do it. The alternative archaeology community’s central claims (that the oldest layers at megalithic sites are the most technically sophisticated, that tool marks on stone surfaces imply technologies beyond the attributed culture’s capability, that the global distribution of construction anomalies implies a common origin) are observational rather than mathematical. They can be verified by visiting the sites and examining the evidence, but “visiting the sites and examining the evidence” requires resources, expertise, and access that the mainstream has no institutional incentive to provide.

Mandelbrot’s Invisible College became visible because mathematics is self-validating: the proof is the proof, regardless of who produces it. The alternative archaeology Invisible College may remain invisible because its evidence is site-specific: the proof is in the stone, and the stone is in Egypt, Peru, Turkey, Japan, and two dozen other locations that require travel, permits, equipment, and time. The evidence cannot be verified from a desk. It requires boots on the ground, and the institutional structure that controls access to the ground has, so far, shown limited interest in granting access to researchers whose conclusions might complicate the existing narrative.

There is one more feature of the network worth addressing before closing, because it is the feature that makes the Invisible College different from every other informal scientific community on record.

The network includes people who disagree with each other on fundamental questions.

This is unusual. Most informal scientific networks are organized around agreement. The people who gather at a conference on string theory are, for the most part, people who believe string theory is a productive framework. The people who gather at a conference on climate science are, for the most part, people who accept the basic findings of climate science. Disagreement exists within these communities, but it is disagreement about details, not foundations.

The Invisible College of alternative archaeology includes people who disagree about foundations. Hancock believes the builders were human. Sklyarov believed they were extraterrestrial. This is not a detail. It is the central question of the field, and the two most prolific researchers in the field answered it in opposite directions. West was interested in the Sphinx. Dunn was interested in machining tolerances. Bauval was interested in astronomical alignments. Sitchin was interested in Sumerian texts. Schoch was interested in geology. Each brought a different disciplinary lens. Each reached different conclusions. And yet all of them participated in the same citation network, referenced each other’s work, and treated each other’s evidence (if not each other’s interpretations) as worth engaging with.

This tolerance for foundational disagreement is the Invisible College’s most distinctive and most underappreciated feature. It means that the network is not a community of believers. It is a community of investigators who happen to be investigating the same anomalies and who have found, through the network’s informal structure, a way to share evidence without requiring agreement about what the evidence means.

The sharing operates through a specific mechanism: the separation of observation from interpretation. When Sklyarov cited Dunn’s Serapeum measurements, he was citing the observation (the interior walls of the granite sarcophagus show no light gap when tested with a precision straightedge) and not the interpretation (the Great Pyramid is a power plant). When Hancock cited Schoch’s erosion analysis, he was citing the observation (the Sphinx shows water-erosion patterns) and not the interpretation (the Sphinx was carved during the Younger Dryas). The network functions because its members have learned to treat evidence as a shared resource and interpretation as a private matter. The stone is common ground. The explanation is where each researcher is alone.

This is, in a certain light, a more honest arrangement than the one that prevails in academic science, where the pressure to agree with your department’s theoretical framework is real and the cost of public disagreement is professional. In the Invisible College, there is no department, no framework, and no cost. You can use Dunn’s measurements and reject his pyramid-as-power-plant hypothesis without losing your office, your graduate students, or your funding, because you have none of these things to begin with.

The freedom is real, but it comes at a price: the absence of the institutional infrastructure that turns individual observations into collective knowledge. In academic science, individual observations are aggregated through journals, conferences, peer review, and meta-analyses into a body of knowledge that transcends any single researcher. In the Invisible College, individual observations remain attached to individual researchers. Dunn’s measurements belong to Dunn. Schoch’s erosion analysis belongs to Schoch. Sklyarov’s stratification principle belongs to Sklyarov. There is no mechanism for aggregating these observations into a single, communally owned body of evidence that could be evaluated as a whole.

This is the Invisible College’s greatest weakness. The evidence exists. It is extensive. It spans six continents, multiple disciplines, and several decades. But it is scattered across forty books in two languages, dozens of websites, hundreds of blog posts, and an uncounted number of YouTube presentations. No one has compiled it into a single, rigorous, peer-reviewed document that a mainstream scientist could read and evaluate without spending years tracking down the sources. The evidence is invisible not because it is hidden but because it is dispersed.

Let me close with a scene, because the scene captures something the analysis does not.

In September 2013, Graham Hancock visited Gobekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey. He spent three days at the site with Klaus Schmidt, the German archaeologist who had been excavating it since 1994. Schmidt was a mainstream archaeologist in every sense: university-trained, institutionally employed, publishing in peer-reviewed journals. His excavation had produced the most important archaeological discovery of the late twentieth century: a megalithic site carbon-dated to approximately 9600 BCE, older than any previously known monumental construction by over six thousand years. And the quality of the oldest enclosures was the highest. The oldest was the best.

Schmidt told Hancock: “The oldest is the best.” He said it, by Hancock’s account, with the matter-of-fact tone of a scientist reporting an observation that contradicted everything his discipline expected. The oldest should not have been the best. Monumental construction should appear at the end of a developmental sequence, not the beginning. Gobekli Tepe inverted the sequence, and Schmidt knew it, and he said so.

Schmidt died in 2014. He never had the opportunity to fully publish his interpretation of what Gobekli Tepe meant for the conventional timeline of human civilization. Hancock, in his books and interviews, has carried Schmidt’s observation into a global audience that Schmidt himself never reached. This is the Invisible College at work: a mainstream excavator’s finding, transmitted through an alternative researcher’s network, reaching an audience that the excavator’s own institutional channels would not have served.

Six years earlier, in October 2007, Andrey Sklyarov was in Peru, documenting disk-saw marks at Ollantaytambo: a grid of parallel cuts, two to three millimeters wide, with identical spacing, consistent with a large rotating blade. He was traveling with a team that included physicists and geophysicists. They measured the cuts. They photographed them. They compared them to identical marks they had documented at Egyptian sites in 2004. They noted the consistency: the same cut width, the same spacing, the same material (granite and basalt), on two continents separated by ten thousand kilometers with no known cultural contact.

Sklyarov did not know about Hancock’s visit to Gobekli Tepe, which had not yet occurred. Hancock did not know about Sklyarov’s measurements at Ollantaytambo, which were published in Russian. Neither researcher knew that the other was, at roughly the same period in their careers, gathering the physical evidence that would become the most frequently cited data in their respective corpora. They were working in the same field, studying the same anomalies, building on many of the same sources, and they were invisible to each other.

The Invisible College is not a metaphor. It is a description. These researchers formed a community without knowing they were a community, produced a distributed research program without coordinating it, and assembled a body of evidence that no single institution has yet attempted to evaluate. The seventeenth-century Invisible College became the Royal Society. Whether the alternative archaeology Invisible College becomes anything at all will depend not on the quality of its evidence (which exists and is extensive) but on whether the institutional structure of modern science can develop a mechanism for engaging with evidence that comes from outside its walls.

The mechanism does not currently exist. The evidence, in the meantime, continues to accumulate on bookshelves in Tucson, on the Laboratory of Alternative History website, in Russian-language PDFs that no Anglophone archaeologist has read, and in the memories of a retired geologist who once pressed a straightedge against the interior wall of a granite sarcophagus in the Serapeum and saw no light.
