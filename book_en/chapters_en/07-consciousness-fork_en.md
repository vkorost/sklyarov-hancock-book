# Chapter 7: The Orthogonal Absence

In January 2003, Graham Hancock drank ayahuasca for the first time.

He was in the Amazon basin, in a wooden ceremonial house with no electricity, sitting on a mat beside eleven other participants. The shaman was a man from the Cofan people of the Ecuador-Colombia border region. The brew was the color of strong tea and tasted, Hancock later said, like something that had been scraped from the floor of a forest and dissolved in bile. He drank approximately half a cup. Within forty minutes, the visions began.

First came geometric patterns: grids, spirals, interlocking hexagons that pulsed with color and shifted like kaleidoscope fragments assembling themselves into an architecture he could not name. Then the geometry resolved into something else. Serpents. Not metaphorical serpents, not the kind of serpents you see when you close your eyes too tightly against a headache. Enormous, vivid, three-dimensional serpentine entities that occupied the space around him with what he described as intelligence and purpose. They were aware of him. They communicated, though not in words. They showed him things: scenes from his life replayed with a moral commentary he had not requested, failures of kindness and courage presented without accusation but with a clarity that made him weep.

Hancock was fifty-two years old. He had written Fingerprints of the Gods, Keeper of Genesis, The Mars Mystery, and Underworld. He had spent a decade building the most detailed alternative account of human prehistory in the English language. He had mapped the megalithic anomalies of six continents, traced the mythological patterns of twenty civilizations, dived on submerged ruins in Japan and India, and developed a civilizational hypothesis that rested on evidence, astronomy, and comparative mythology. He was, by any measure, one of the most widely read investigators of the ancient world alive.

And now he was lying on a mat in the Amazon, watching serpent beings rearrange his understanding of reality.

The experience changed him. Not in the dramatic, overnight, road-to-Damascus sense (though Hancock himself might argue otherwise), but in the way that a new instrument changes a scientist. It gave him something he had not previously had: a methodology for investigating consciousness directly, rather than inferring it from its architectural and mythological residues. Before ayahuasca, Hancock’s work was about stones, maps, and myths. After ayahuasca, it was about all of those things plus the proposition that consciousness itself might be a domain of investigation as real and as structured as the archaeological record.

By 2005, when he published Supernatural: Meetings with the Ancient Teachers of Mankind, the pivot was complete. Hancock had moved from investigating what the ancients built to investigating what the ancients experienced. The geometric forms of ayahuasca visions, he argued, matched the geometric forms painted on cave walls at Lascaux, Chauvet, and Altamira thirty thousand years earlier. The serpentine entities he encountered in the Amazon were the same serpentine entities depicted in ancient art on every continent. The shamanic tradition, in which trained practitioners used plant medicines to access a visionary domain populated by intelligent beings, was not primitive superstition. It was, in Hancock’s new model, the oldest science: a systematic, repeatable investigation of a real phenomenon, conducted over millennia by people who had developed the most sophisticated pharmacological knowledge on the planet.

By 2019, in America Before, he had gone further. “My speculation,” he wrote, “which I will not attempt to prove here or to support with evidence but merely present for consideration, is that the advanced civilization I see evolving in North America during the Ice Age had transcended leverage and mechanical advantage and learned to manipulate matter and energy by deploying powers of consciousness that we have not yet begun to tap.” The lost civilization was not merely a society of astronomers and navigators. It was a society of shamans who had developed consciousness itself into a technology.

By 2024, in his Lex Fridman interview, the integration was total. “I think shamanism is the origin of everything of value in humanity,” he said. “I think it was the earliest form of science.” The lost civilization, he explained, was “a civilization like all civilizations that emerged out of shamanism, but that went a little bit further.” Consciousness was not a side interest. It was the foundation.

Now open Andrey Sklyarov’s thirty-two books. All of them. Open the indexes, the chapter headings, the footnotes. Search for the word “consciousness.” Search for “ayahuasca.” Search for “shamanism,” “DMT,” “psilocybin,” “altered states,” “entheogenic,” “psychedelic,” “vision quest,” “meditation,” “mystical experience.”

You will find nothing.

Not a dismissal. Not a critique. Not a footnote explaining why consciousness exploration is irrelevant to the question of ancient construction. Not a single sentence in which Sklyarov considers the possibility that plant medicines might provide useful data about the ancient world, evaluates the proposition, and rejects it. The word “consciousness” does not appear in his corpus in any methodological sense. It is not present the way a competing hypothesis is present: as something weighed, tested, and found wanting. It is absent the way oxygen is absent from the vocabulary of a fish.

This is the divergence I want to name.

In every chapter of this book so far, the disagreements between Hancock and Sklyarov have been disagreements. Two researchers look at the same stone, the same myth, the same sarcophagus, and reach different conclusions. The Species Question is a disagreement about who built the monuments. The Maintenance Manual is a disagreement about what religion is. These are arguments between two positions, and the arguments are productive because the positions share a common evidential ground.

What I am describing now is different. This is not a disagreement. It is a gap so total that the two research programs do not even occupy the same conceptual space. Hancock’s post-2005 work takes place in a world where consciousness is a primary phenomenon, where plant medicines provide access to a real domain of experience, where shamanism is the oldest science, and where the lost civilization’s greatest achievement may have been the development of consciousness-based technologies that we cannot yet replicate. Sklyarov’s work takes place in a world where none of those categories exist. Not as rejected hypotheses. Not as interesting possibilities that the evidence fails to support. As absent categories.

I call this the Orthogonal Absence. It is the condition in which one researcher’s central concern is not merely peripheral to the other researcher but is entirely missing from his intellectual universe. Not contradicted. Not refuted. Not even visible. The two research programs are not parallel lines that never meet. They are perpendicular lines: each one extends infinitely in a direction the other does not recognize as a dimension.

The Orthogonal Absence is different from ordinary disagreement in a specific way. When two researchers disagree, you can map the disagreement: here is the evidence, here are the two interpretations, and the distance between them is measurable. When one researcher’s central lens is entirely absent from the other’s world, there is no shared axis along which to measure the distance. Hancock could no more explain to Sklyarov why ayahuasca matters than a painter could explain color to someone who not only has never seen it but has no concept that seeing involves light.

This is not a failure on Sklyarov’s part. That is the point I need to make carefully, because the temptation is to read the Orthogonal Absence as a deficit: Sklyarov was missing something; he should have explored consciousness; his system would have been richer if he had. This reading is wrong, and it is wrong for a reason that goes deeper than intellectual charity.

In 1903, the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov won the Nobel Prize for his work on digestive physiology. Pavlov’s discovery of classical conditioning (ring a bell before feeding a dog, and eventually the bell alone will produce salivation) became one of the foundational findings of behavioral psychology. But what is less well known outside Russia is the intellectual context that produced Pavlov’s work: the Russian physiological tradition of Ivan Sechenov, who published Reflexes of the Brain in 1863.

Sechenov’s central argument was that all psychic activity, without exception, could be explained as reflex arcs: stimulus, nerve transmission, response. Thought, emotion, will, consciousness: all were, in Sechenov’s account, elaborate reflexes operating through the nervous system. There was no need for a separate category called “mind” or “consciousness” that existed independently of brain activity. The brain was a machine. The machine operated by physical laws. What we called consciousness was the machine’s operation, observed from the inside.

This was not a marginal position. It became, through Pavlov and his successors, the backbone of Russian and Soviet psychology. When the Soviet state adopted dialectical materialism as its official philosophy, Sechenov’s reflex theory was already waiting to be elevated into doctrine. Consciousness was secondary. Matter was primary. The brain produced thoughts the way the liver produced bile: through physical processes that could be measured, analyzed, and predicted. To propose that consciousness was an independent phenomenon, a primary substance, a domain that could be investigated through subjective experience, was to commit a philosophical error so basic that it did not even need to be refuted. It simply was not science.

This tradition did not produce monsters. It produced Pavlov. It produced Luria, whose neuropsychological case studies remain foundational. It produced Vygotsky, whose developmental psychology is taught in every education department in the world. It produced a century of rigorous, materialist investigation of the brain and behavior that generated real knowledge. The tradition’s limitation was not that it was wrong (about much, it was right) but that it defined certain questions as outside the boundaries of science, and once defined as outside, those questions became invisible. Not forbidden, exactly. Invisible. A Russian-trained scientist did not refuse to investigate consciousness as a primary phenomenon. He simply did not encounter the category.

Sklyarov was trained in physics, not psychology, but the intellectual atmosphere was the same. The Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology, where he earned his degree, was not a place where students debated the ontological status of consciousness over lunch. It was a place where students solved differential equations, calibrated instruments, and learned to measure the world with a precision that left no room for categories that could not be measured. When Sklyarov graduated and began his investigation of ancient anomalies, he carried this atmospheric residue into his work the way all of us carry the air of the places that formed us.

He did not reject ayahuasca. He did not dismiss shamanism. He did not argue against the proposition that consciousness might be a primary phenomenon. He simply operated in a world where those ideas did not constitute propositions. They were not wrong. They were not thinkable.

I want to be precise about this, because there is a moment in Sklyarov’s early career that, at first glance, appears to contradict what I have just said.

In 2000, Sklyarov published his second book, Fundamentals of Spirit Physics. It was his most philosophically ambitious work: a complete ontological system in which matter and spirit were co-equal primary substrates of the universe. He called this “dual first-substance.” He built the architecture using analogies to quantum physics, the EPR paradox, superstring theory. He proposed that information was a form of energy, that the soul had a hierarchical structure, that resonance and dissonance governed the interaction between matter and spirit. He explicitly rejected both pure materialism and pure idealism. He was, in this single book, closer to Hancock’s intellectual territory than at any other point in his career.

And then the book vanished from his work. Not retracted. Not criticized. Not superseded by a better theory. It simply was never referenced again. The dual-first-substance system, the pyramid of the soul, the resonance-dissonance model: none of these appear in any of the thirty subsequent books. In 2016, in his final work, Sklyarov included a brief chapter titled “A Little About the Spiritual…” which acknowledged the spiritual dimension and then declined to develop it further. Sixteen years of silence bookended by a single early experiment and a brief, valedictory nod.

What happened?

The 2004 Egypt expedition happened. Sklyarov, who by that year was measuring sarcophagus tolerances in the Serapeum and documenting saw marks at Aswan, found himself confronted for the first time with physical evidence he could touch, measure, and photograph. And the evidence was so compelling, so unambiguously material, that it pulled him entirely into the domain where his training was strongest.

The spirit-matter ontology was the road not taken. It reveals something about Sklyarov’s intellectual character: the willingness, early in his career, to think at the boundary between physics and metaphysics. But it also reveals the gravitational pull of material evidence. When Sklyarov found his evidence, the evidence was stone. Not vision. Not experience. Not the geometry of an ayahuasca ceremony. Stone. Granite. Basalt. Diorite. Materials you can measure with a micrometer and analyze with a mass spectrometer. The road that led toward spirit was abandoned not because it was wrong but because the road that led toward stone was so much more tractable, so much more suited to the instruments Sklyarov carried.

Hancock, at roughly the same moment in his career, found a different kind of evidence. Not stone but experience. Not measurement but testimony. The ayahuasca ceremony produced data of a different kind: consistent visual phenomena across subjects, recurring entity encounters, geometric forms matching ancient art. Hancock processed this data with the instruments he carried: narrative, pattern recognition, comparative analysis. Both researchers found evidence that pulled them deeper into their respective domains. Both followed the pull. And the pull carried them in perpendicular directions.

Sklyarov’s analytical framework was complete. His reasoning was rigorous. His evidence was physical, measurable, and well-documented. But his approach had no mechanism for processing a certain category of evidence: the experiential, the subjective, the kind of data that ayahuasca produces. This is not because he was analytically impaired. It is because his intellectual tradition defined that category of data as outside the domain of legitimate inquiry. The tradition was coherent and productive. It generated real knowledge. And it was blind to an entire dimension of the phenomenon it was investigating.

Hancock’s approach, conversely, was rich in experiential and subjective data but sometimes struggled to generate the kind of precise, falsifiable predictions that Sklyarov’s method produced easily. Hancock could describe the serpent entities of the ayahuasca vision with extraordinary vividness. He could map their correspondence to ancient art with genuine erudition. But he could not tell you the dielectric constant of the Ark of the Covenant, and Sklyarov could.

Each system was complete within its own domain. Each was blind to the other’s domain. The blindness was not a defect. It was a feature of the instruments each man carried.

There is a specific way in which Hancock and Sklyarov processed the same mythological content that makes the Orthogonal Absence visible at the level of a single image.

Both researchers were familiar with the serpent beings of global mythology. The Nagas of Indian tradition: serpentine, intelligent, possessing their own civilization beneath the earth’s surface. The feathered serpent Quetzalcoatl of Mesoamerica. The Uraeus serpent of Egyptian royal iconography. The serpent in Genesis. The dragon-kings of Chinese cosmology. The rainbow serpent of Aboriginal Australian tradition. Serpentine figures appear in the mythology and art of virtually every human culture, and both Hancock and Sklyarov recognized this global distribution as a pattern requiring explanation.

Hancock’s explanation, developed after his ayahuasca experiences, was that the serpent beings of mythology were the same entities encountered in altered states of consciousness. The shamans of the Upper Paleolithic who painted therianthropic (half-human, half-animal) figures on the walls of Lascaux and Chauvet were recording what they saw in trance states. The serpent entities depicted across world cultures were real beings, inhabitants of a domain accessible through plant medicines and shamanic practice, who had been interacting with human consciousness for tens of thousands of years. The neurological universality of the serpent motif (David Lewis-Williams at the University of the Witwatersrand had demonstrated that entoptic phenomena, including serpentine forms, are generated by the human nervous system under altered-state conditions) was not evidence that the entities were mere hallucination. It was evidence that the human brain was, under certain conditions, consistently accessing the same non-ordinary reality.

Sklyarov’s explanation was that the serpent beings of mythology were a physical alien species. The Nagas were literally non-human beings with their own civilization. The reptilian figures in Chinese, Indian, and Mesoamerican art were documentary depictions of a biological species that had once lived on or visited Earth. In his early book Introduction to Dragonography (approximately early 2000s), Sklyarov proposed that intelligent reptilian extraterrestrials were the historical prototypes for dragon and serpent myths worldwide. He later de-emphasized this particular line of inquiry (the reptilian hypothesis does not appear prominently in his mature work, which focused on the humanoid gods), but the intellectual move was characteristic: where Hancock saw a visionary entity accessed through consciousness, Sklyarov saw a physical organism documented in historical texts.

Same serpent. Same global distribution. Same cultural persistence across millennia. Two readings that do not contradict each other so much as inhabit different universes. In Hancock’s universe, the serpent is something you encounter when you drink ayahuasca. In Sklyarov’s universe, the serpent is something you encounter when you read a Sumerian text carefully and apply the principle that mythological entities had physical referents. The serpent is, simultaneously, a vision and a species, an experience and a fossil, a door and a body. The two readings cannot see each other, because each one occupies a dimension the other does not recognize.

I want to push on this a little further, because the Orthogonal Absence is not merely a curiosity about two researchers. It is a window into something larger about how intellectual traditions shape the questions their practitioners can ask.

Consider the pharmacological fact at the center of Hancock’s consciousness argument: the ayahuasca brew itself. The brew is made from two plants. The vine Banisteriopsis caapi contains beta-carboline monoamine oxidase inhibitors. The shrub Psychotria viridis (or other DMT-containing plants) provides dimethyltryptamine. Neither plant, consumed alone, produces the characteristic ayahuasca experience. The DMT is normally destroyed in the gut by monoamine oxidase enzymes before it reaches the brain. Only the combination works: the MAO inhibitor from the vine blocks the enzyme that would destroy the DMT, allowing it to cross the blood-brain barrier intact.

This is not a trivial pharmacological feat. Among the estimated eighty thousand plant species in the Amazon, the Amazonian shamans identified the specific combination that produces a neurochemically precise result. Hancock calls this “a remarkable scientific feat” and uses it to argue that shamanic practice represents genuine empirical investigation, conducted over centuries or millennia, producing real pharmacological knowledge.

Sklyarov, in his treatment of Amazonian and Andean plant knowledge, focused on a different kind of botanical sophistication: the multi-step detoxification processes for toxic tubers, the complex chemistry of making bitter potatoes edible. He cited the same data that Hancock cited (drawn partly from Hancock’s own sources) about the anomalous sophistication of ancient Andean botanical knowledge. But his explanation was different: the gods imposed this knowledge as part of their agricultural program, providing finished protocols to their human workforce the way a factory manager provides operating procedures to assembly-line workers.

Notice what Sklyarov did not do. He did not turn his botanical analysis toward the ayahuasca brew itself. He did not ask: if the gods transmitted agricultural knowledge, did they also transmit pharmacological knowledge? Did the ayahuasca formula come from the same source as the potato-detoxification protocols? Was the brew a technology of the gods, designed for a purpose that the gods understood and the humans preserved in ritual form?

This is precisely the kind of question that Sklyarov’s framework was designed to ask. He asked it about the Ark of the Covenant (technology preserved in ritual form). He asked it about the lingam (technology preserved in ritual form). He asked it about the menorah, the djed column, the was-scepter (all technology preserved in ritual form). His Maintenance Manual lens was a universal tool for re-reading ritual objects as degraded technology. But he never applied it to ayahuasca. He never asked: is the ayahuasca ceremony a Maintenance Manual entry for a pharmacological device?

The question was available. The lens was waiting. And the question was never asked, because the lens could only process objects. It could not process experiences. A sarcophagus is an object. A brew is an object. But the experience produced by the brew is not an object, and Sklyarov’s method had no category for processing data that arrived as experience rather than as measurement.

There is a story, probably apocryphal but instructive, about a Soviet delegation visiting a Western neuropsychology laboratory in the 1970s. The Western researchers had developed a protocol for studying meditation: EEG recordings of experienced meditators during deep practice, showing distinctive patterns of brain activity that differed from both waking and sleeping states. The Soviet delegation examined the equipment, reviewed the data, and asked polite questions about methodology. At the end of the visit, one of the Soviet scientists reportedly said, “This is very interesting. But in our tradition, we study what the brain does, not what the person thinks the brain does.”

The remark, whether real or invented, captures something about the intellectual partition that the Orthogonal Absence describes. For the Western researchers, the meditator’s subjective report was data. The experience of calm, of expanded awareness, of contact with something beyond ordinary consciousness: these were phenomena to be investigated. For the Soviet visitor, the subjective report was not data. It was noise. The data was the EEG trace: the objective, measurable, third-person record of what the brain was doing while the person claimed to be having an experience. The experience itself was epiphenomenal, a by-product of physical processes, interesting only to the extent that it could be correlated with measurable neural activity.

Sklyarov did not study meditation. But he operated within the same partition. The ancient world, in his framework, was full of physical processes that produced effects humans interpreted as spiritual experiences. The Ark’s radiation produced fear and awe. The djed column’s resonance produced sound that participants interpreted as divine communication. The menorah’s light produced an atmosphere that participants experienced as sacred. In every case, the physical process was real and the experiential interpretation was a later human addition. The experience was the noise. The physics was the signal.

Hancock’s model inverted this hierarchy. The experience was the signal. The physics was secondary, or perhaps instrumental: the brew, the ceremony, the specific botanical combination were the technology through which the experience was accessed, but the experience itself was primary. The serpent entities were real. The moral instruction they delivered was real. The geometric domain they inhabited was real. The DMT was merely the key that opened the door.

Two hierarchies. Same ancient world. Same serpents on the same walls. One reads the serpents as a record of what ancient people saw when they altered their brain chemistry. The other reads the serpents as a record of what ancient people saw when they encountered a non-human species. Neither reading is refuted by the evidence. Both are consistent with the physical record. And the choice between them is not made at the level of evidence. It is made at the level of what counts as evidence.

I want to be fair to both sides of this partition, because it is easy to romanticize the consciousness model and to caricature the materialist one.

Hancock’s consciousness lens generates a powerful reading of the ancient world. It explains why the same geometric motifs appear in cave art across continents and millennia: the human nervous system generates them reliably under altered-state conditions, and shamans worldwide accessed and recorded them. It explains why serpentine entities appear in virtually every mythology: they are consistent features of the DMT experience, encountered by any practitioner who achieves sufficient depth. It explains why initiation rituals worldwide follow the same death-and-rebirth pattern: they are records of what the initiate experiences when consciousness is temporarily freed from its ordinary filtering function.

It also generates predictions. If the brain-as-receiver hypothesis is correct, then the DMTx extended-state trials at Imperial College London and the University of California San Diego should produce consistent entity encounters across subjects, consistent geometric environments, and consistent moral content. As of Hancock’s 2024 interviews, preliminary results appear consistent with these predictions (consistent entity encounters, consistent visual environments, consistent reports of moral instruction from the entities), though the trials are ongoing and peer-reviewed publications are still emerging.

Sklyarov’s materialist architecture generates a different kind of power. It produces specific, falsifiable claims about the physical world: the composition of tool-mark residues, the construction tolerances of sarcophagi, the alloy composition of ancient metal clamps. It converts mythological texts into engineering hypotheses that can, in principle, be tested. The Ark-as-capacitor prediction is testable (calculate the dielectric properties of dried acacia wood at the specified dimensions). The nickel-cobalt tool-residue finding is falsifiable (analyze more surfaces, compare more sites).

What the materialist approach cannot do is account for the experiential dimension of the ancient world. If ancient peoples consistently encountered serpentine entities during shamanic practice, and if those encounters consistently delivered moral instruction, and if the practice reliably produced pharmacologically precise altered states, then these are data. The data are real even if the entities are not. The consistency of the experience across cultures and millennia is a phenomenon that requires explanation. And a structure that has no category for subjective experience cannot provide that explanation, any more than a structure that has no category for mass spectrometry can identify the composition of a tool-mark residue.

The Orthogonal Absence is not a judgment about which approach is better. It is an observation about what each approach cannot see. And what each approach cannot see is, in both cases, something real.

There is one more layer to this, and it is the most personal.

Hancock’s turn toward consciousness was not purely intellectual. It was experiential. He drank ayahuasca. He had the visions. He encountered the entities. He wept. Whatever one thinks about the ontological status of the serpent beings, the experience of encountering them was, for Hancock, as real as the experience of standing at the base of the Great Pyramid. Perhaps more real, because the pyramid is external and the vision is internal, and the internal is the only domain where certainty is not a metaphor.

In 2017, Hancock suffered a seizure. He was on a plane, traveling with his wife Santha. His heart stopped. He was clinically dead for a brief period before being resuscitated. In his Lex Fridman interview, he described this as a near-death experience that deepened his conviction that consciousness is not reducible to brain activity. “I’m not afraid of death at all,” he said. “I’m curious about death. I think it could be very interesting. I think it’s the beginning of the next great adventure.”

This is not the kind of statement that can be evaluated scientifically. It is not a hypothesis. It is not a data point. It is a man telling you what it feels like to have died briefly and come back, and what that experience did to his understanding of what consciousness is and where it goes. You can accept it or reject it, but you cannot measure it.

Sklyarov’s work, by contrast, contains no personal confessions. He does not tell you how he felt when he first pressed a ruler against the interior of the Serapeum sarcophagus. He does not describe the emotional impact of seeing polygonal masonry at Alaca Huyuk that matched, to the centimeter, the masonry he had documented in Cusco five years earlier. He does not share his inner life. His books are structures of argument and evidence, built with the impersonal precision of the engineering he admired. The person behind the framework is visible only in the framework’s architecture: its rigor, its thoroughness, its systematic coverage of evidence across thirty-two books, and its total silence on the subject of inner experience.

This silence is itself a kind of data. It tells you that for Sklyarov, the inner life was not a domain of inquiry. It was private, perhaps. It was irrelevant to the investigation, certainly. It was not something you brought to the page, because the page was for evidence, and evidence was external. The world was out there, in the stones, in the texts, in the chemical composition of tool-mark residues. What was in here, inside the investigator, was not the subject of the investigation. It was the investigator, doing his job, measuring the world.

Hancock brought his inner life to every page. His vulnerability, his uncertainty, his willingness to say “I don’t know” and “I was wrong” and “this experience changed me.” His books are first-person investigations in which the investigator’s transformation is part of the evidence. The turn toward ayahuasca was not a methodological decision made in an office. It was a man walking into a ceremonial house in the Amazon and allowing his consciousness to be altered by a brew he did not understand, in the company of strangers, under the guidance of a shaman whose language he did not speak, because the alternative was to never know what was on the other side of the door.

Sklyarov would not have walked through that door. Not because he was a coward (he led expeditions to remote and occasionally dangerous archaeological sites on six continents) but because the door, in his intellectual universe, was not there. There was no door. There was a wall, and behind the wall was nothing that physics could measure, and if physics could not measure it, it was not a datum. It was atmosphere. It was decoration. It was the noise that humans added to the signal after the signal’s source departed.

Consider what happens when a hospital chaplain and a neurosurgeon stand at the same bedside. The chaplain sees a soul in crisis, a person whose suffering has spiritual meaning and whose recovery depends partly on hope, connection, and the willingness to find purpose in pain. The neurosurgeon sees a lesion on a scan, a set of symptoms with a differential diagnosis, and a surgical approach that will either succeed or fail based on anatomy and technique. Both are present to the same patient. Both believe they are dealing with real forces. Both would find the other’s framework, if pressed, incomplete at best.

The Orthogonal Absence between Hancock and Sklyarov is this kind of partition, operating at the level of two individual researchers rather than two communities. Each man’s framework is coherent, productive, and invisible to the other. Each man’s central concern, the thing that keeps him working through decades of institutional indifference, is something the other man’s system has no category for.

For Hancock, the central concern is consciousness: what it is, where it comes from, whether it survives death, whether it can be expanded to access domains of reality ordinarily filtered out by the brain’s default function. This concern drives his investigation of the ancient world, because the ancient world, in his reading, was built by people who understood consciousness better than we do.

For Sklyarov, the central concern is the material identity of the builders: what they were made of, what tools they used, what metallurgical traditions they practiced, what physical processes they employed. This concern drives his investigation of the ancient world, because the ancient world, in his reading, contains physical evidence of a non-human technology that can be identified by its residues.

Both concerns are legitimate. Both generate real knowledge. Neither can see the other. And the space between them is not a gap to be bridged but a geometry to be recognized: two lines extending in directions perpendicular to each other, each one infinite within its own plane, each one a single point from the other’s perspective.

This is the Orthogonal Absence. It is the deepest asymmetry between two researchers who agreed on eighty percent of the physical evidence and could not even disagree on the rest, because the rest occupied dimensions that only one of them could see.

There is a question that this chapter raises but cannot answer, and I want to state it plainly before closing.

If Sklyarov had drunk ayahuasca, what would he have seen?

This is not a frivolous question. The pharmacology is real. DMT reliably produces visual phenomena, entity encounters, and geometric environments in controlled clinical settings. The experience is consistent across demographics, cultures, and belief systems. Atheists report entity encounters. Materialists report entity encounters. The serpent beings and geometric grids do not care about your intellectual commitments. They appear regardless.

If Sklyarov, a physicist trained at MIPT, a man whose entire career was built on the principle that every phenomenon has a material substrate, had ingested a pharmacologically active compound and encountered serpentine entities in a geometric domain, what would his model have done with the data?

The most likely outcome is that his framework would have processed the experience the way it processed every other anomaly: by looking for the material substrate. What is the neurochemical mechanism? What physical property of the DMT molecule interacts with which receptor to produce which pattern of neural firing? The experience itself, the visions, the entities, the moral instruction, would have been classified as the brain’s response to a chemical stimulus, the way nausea is the stomach’s response to a toxin. Real, in the sense that the subject genuinely experiences it. But not evidence of anything beyond the brain’s chemistry under altered conditions.

Hancock would disagree. He would say that the entities are not the brain’s production but the brain’s reception: that DMT lowers the filter, and what comes through the lowered filter is something that was already there, waiting. The brain-as-receiver hypothesis, which Hancock has articulated with increasing confidence since 2005, holds that consciousness is not generated by the brain but received by it, “just as a television set is the receiver of television signals.” Psychedelics do not create the signal. They tune the receiver to a frequency that is normally blocked.

Sklyarov, had he lived to hear this hypothesis, would have asked: what is the transmission medium? What physical process carries the signal? What is the bandwidth? What is the power source? These are legitimate questions. They are the questions a physicist asks. And they are questions that the brain-as-receiver hypothesis, in its current formulation, cannot answer, because the hypothesis is a metaphor, not a physical model.

But Hancock would respond: the ancient civilizations that built the megalithic sites, the shamanic traditions that persist in the Amazon, the Hermetic and Gnostic initiatory traditions that survived two millennia of persecution, the cave artists of Lascaux and Chauvet: these all suggest that a technology for accessing the signal exists, that it has been practiced for at least thirty thousand years, and that the signal delivers consistent content (geometric forms, entity encounters, moral instruction) across cultures and centuries. The consistency is the evidence. The mechanism is unknown. But unknown is not nonexistent.

The question itself, “What would Sklyarov have seen?”, is a question that could not arise within Sklyarov’s intellectual system. It is visible only from the outside. And the fact that it is visible from the outside, that there is an outside from which the limitation can be seen, is itself evidence that no single lens is sufficient to contain the ancient world.

The stones are real. The visions are real. The serpents on the walls are real, whether they depict entities encountered in altered states or organisms documented in historical texts. The Orthogonal Absence tells us that the ancient world is larger than any one investigator’s instruments can measure, and that the most honest response to its immensity is not to choose one instrument and declare the others irrelevant but to notice, with humility, how much of the world each instrument cannot see.
